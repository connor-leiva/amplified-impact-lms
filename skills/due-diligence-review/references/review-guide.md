# Review Guide

Last verified: 2026-11-08 (the radon action level and the seller concession point; everything else here is review method, not a state rule)

How to read the stack, how to sort findings, and what to look for in each document. The tiers describe the type of problem, not the cost. A missing smoke alarm is Tier 1 and cheap; a tired roof is Tier 2 and expensive.

## Reading Order

1. The signed purchase contract first: the state, the form and its version, the due diligence or inspection deadline, and any addendum that changes it.
2. The inspection report: the summary, then the full section behind every summary item, then every other page. Inspectors sometimes put significant items only in the body.
3. Every other report and document, in full.

**Summary gap check.** Keep a list of every body finding that would be Tier 1 or Tier 2 but is missing from the inspector's summary. Mark each one "Not in the inspector's summary" with its page. These are the findings an agent skimming the summary would miss, so they lead the issue list within their tier.

Track the page for every finding. Use the page numbers printed on the report; if none are printed, use the PDF page and say so.

## Severity Tiers

**Tier 1: Safety and Health.** Conditions that could hurt someone or make the home unsafe to occupy.
- Gas leaks or improper gas connections
- Carbon monoxide risk: cracked heat exchanger, improper venting, a water heater or furnace that backdrafts, flame rollout
- Exposed or energized wiring, scorched, melted or overheating electrical components, a panel the inspector calls hazardous
- Missing or inoperable smoke or CO alarms
- Active water intrusion with suspected mold
- Radon at or above the EPA action level of 4 pCi/L
- Structural conditions the inspector calls unsafe
- Fall hazards such as missing stair or deck railings

**Tier 2: Major System or Structure.** Expensive systems and the building itself.
- Roof at or near the end of its life, or an active leak
- Foundation cracks with displacement, or signs of active movement
- Grading or drainage that sends water toward the foundation, with evidence of moisture
- Sewer line defects from a scope: bellies, offsets, root intrusion, breaks
- Furnace, air conditioner, or water heater that is failing or has defects
- Electrical capacity problems or panel concerns the inspector raises
- Plumbing leaks or supply problems the inspector raises
- Significant failure of windows, siding, or the building envelope

**Tier 3: Repair and Maintenance.** Real but routine.
- Minor leaks, missing GFCI protection at a few outlets, caulking and sealing
- Small roof or gutter repairs, doors that stick, minor grading touch-ups
- Service recommendations, such as an HVAC cleaning

**Tier 4: Informational.** Wear, cosmetics, age notes on systems that work, general maintenance advice.

### Age Versus Defect

An old system that works isn't a defect. A 20-year-old furnace that runs safely belongs in the buyer's budget planning and may support a credit conversation or a home warranty, but it isn't a repair request. Call it Tier 2 only when the inspector reports a defect or failure. Never estimate remaining life unless the report states one.

### "Further Evaluation Recommended"

Rate the underlying concern at the tier it implies, and push for the specialist's evaluation inside the due diligence window. An evaluation that comes back after the deadline can't support an objection. If it can't fit, say so and name the choices: ask for the repair by a licensed contractor of that trade, ask for more time in writing, or decide without it.

### The Inspector's Own Words

Keep the inspector's own rating ("safety," "repair," "monitor") when they give one. If you tier an item differently, say why in one line. Quote the inspector when the exact words matter ("scorching observed," "active leak," "end of service life").

## Disclosure Cross-Check

Compare the seller's property condition disclosure against the inspection, item by item where they overlap:
- Water: prior leaks, flooding or moisture versus staining, efflorescence, elevated moisture readings or patched areas in the report
- Roof: the disclosed age and repairs versus the inspector's observations
- Major systems: disclosed ages and service history versus data plates and inspector notes
- Additions, finished basements or remodels versus anything the inspector flags as nonstandard. Flag it; don't conclude that work was unpermitted.
- Pests, radon, mold and environmental items the disclosure marks as known or unknown
- Water rights or shares and the irrigation source, when the disclosure names them

Write each mismatch as a neutral question for the listing agent: "The disclosure notes no known leaks; the inspection shows staining below the upstairs bath on page 22. Is there any history there?" Sellers often don't know. Never accuse.

## Radon

- EPA recommends fixing a home at 4 pCi/L or more, and says to consider fixing between 2 and 4 pCi/L.
- No radon test in the stack: flag it, and note the test has to finish and report before the deadline to be useful. EPA tells buyers to know the radon level of any home they consider and to test if it hasn't been tested. EPA's buyer guide says every radon test should run a minimum of 48 hours, so with less than 48 hours left a test can't finish in time without a signed extension.
- A result at or above 4 pCi/L: Tier 1. The usual ask is mitigation by a certified radon mitigation professional and a post-mitigation test. Never quote a mitigation cost without a bid. Health questions go to a certified radon professional.
- Utah-specific radon facts are in `utah-property-notes.md`. Use them only for a Utah property.

## HOA Documents

- **Dues:** the amount, how often, and what they cover (water, sewer, trash, exterior insurance, snow removal, landscaping)
- **Special assessments:** current, approved, proposed or discussed in the minutes, with amounts and vote dates. Compare any vote date with the contract's Settlement Deadline and with the contract's line on who pays assessments (for Utah, see `utah-due-diligence-timing.md`).
- **Reserves:** the date of the reserve study, recommended versus actual contributions, and the balance or percent funded. An old study or underfunded reserves are a question to ask, not a verdict.
- **Budget:** operating deficits, delinquency levels if shown, and large insurance increases
- **Minutes:** upcoming projects (roofs, siding, asphalt, amenities), litigation, insurance claims, assessment discussions, pending rule changes, rental waitlists
- **Leasing:** rental restrictions, minimum lease terms, short-term rental bans, rental caps and waitlists, and how many units rent now. This is critical if the buyer plans to rent, even years from now. For Utah, see `utah-property-notes.md` (a buyer does not inherit the seller's grandfathered rental status).
- **Use restrictions:** parking, RVs and trailers, pets, exterior changes, home businesses
- **Insurance (condos and townhomes):** what the master policy covers versus what the owner must insure. Send this to the buyer's insurance agent for a quote.
- **Fees at closing:** reinvestment, transfer and setup fees. Show a reinvestment fee as a percentage of the purchase price. For Utah, compare it with the cap in `utah-property-notes.md` and flag anything above it for the title company to confirm; never call a fee unlawful.
- **Litigation:** any that's pending. Flag it for the buyer's lender too, because pending litigation can affect loan eligibility, especially for condos.

## Title Commitment

Scan the requirements and exceptions for:
- Liens, judgments, notices of pending litigation, or an old deed of trust with no release
- Easements that cross the usable part of the lot, especially where the buyer has said they plan to build, fence or add on
- Encroachments or survey matters
- Unpaid assessments
- A recorded notice of a special district, such as a Utah public infrastructure district (see `utah-property-notes.md`)

Flag anything unusual for the title officer. Don't interpret legal effect. Routine payoff of the seller's loan is not an issue; never repeat loan amounts or loan numbers.

## Insurance Cost

Most contracts let the buyer check the cost and availability of homeowner's insurance during due diligence (the Utah REPC lists it). When the report shows an older roof, prior water damage, or an electrical panel the inspector flags, suggest the buyer get a quote from their insurance agent with the findings in hand before the deadline. Never say whether a carrier will insure the home.

## Seller Credits

Loan programs cap seller-paid financing concessions, and a credit over the cap can change the loan. Never state a cap. The agent confirms any credit amount with the buyer's lender before it goes out.

## Fair Housing Reminder

Review documents and property conditions only. Never describe who lives in the community, the area's demographics, school quality, or safety, even when a document or the agent mentions them. For questions about those, point to official sources.
