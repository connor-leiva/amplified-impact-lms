# Review Guide

How to sort findings and what to look for in each document. The tiers describe the type of problem, not the cost. A missing smoke alarm is Tier 1 and cheap; a tired roof is Tier 2 and expensive.

## Severity Tiers

**Tier 1: Safety and Health.** Conditions that could hurt someone or make the home unsafe to occupy.
- Gas leaks or improper gas connections
- Carbon monoxide risk: cracked heat exchanger, improper venting, a water heater or furnace that backdrafts
- Exposed or energized wiring, scorched or overheating electrical components, a panel the inspector calls hazardous
- Missing or inoperable smoke or CO alarms
- Active water intrusion with suspected mold
- Radon at or above 4.0 pCi/L
- Structural conditions the inspector calls unsafe
- Fall hazards such as missing stair or deck railings

**Tier 2: Major System or Structure.** Expensive systems and the building itself.
- Roof at or near the end of its life, or an active leak
- Foundation cracks with displacement, or signs of active movement
- Grading or drainage that sends water toward the foundation
- Sewer line defects from a scope: bellies, offsets, root intrusion, breaks
- Furnace, air conditioner, or water heater that is failing or has defects
- Electrical capacity problems or panel concerns the inspector raises
- Plumbing supply problems the inspector raises, such as low pressure or materials they flag
- Significant failure of windows, siding, or the building envelope

**Tier 3: Repair and Maintenance.** Real but routine.
- Minor leaks, missing GFCI protection at a few outlets, caulking and sealing
- Small roof or gutter repairs, doors that stick, minor grading touch-ups
- Service recommendations, such as an HVAC cleaning

**Tier 4: Informational.** Wear, cosmetics, age notes on systems that work, general maintenance advice.

### Age Versus Defect

An old system that works isn't a defect. A 20-year-old furnace that runs safely belongs in the buyer's budget planning and may support a credit conversation or a home warranty, but it isn't a repair request. Call it Tier 2 only when the inspector reports a defect or failure.

### "Further Evaluation Recommended"

Rate the underlying concern at the tier it implies, and push for the specialist's evaluation inside the due diligence window. An evaluation that comes back after the deadline can't support an objection.

## Disclosure Cross-Check

Compare the Seller Property Condition Disclosure against the inspection, item by item where they overlap:
- Water: prior leaks, flooding, or moisture versus staining, efflorescence, or patched areas in the report
- Roof: the disclosed age and repairs versus the inspector's observations
- Major systems: disclosed ages and service history versus data plates and inspector notes
- Additions, finished basements, or remodels versus anything the inspector flags as unpermitted-looking or nonstandard (flag it; don't conclude that work was unpermitted)
- Pests, radon, mold, and environmental items the disclosure marks as known or unknown

Write each mismatch as a neutral question for the listing agent: "The disclosure notes no known leaks; the inspection shows staining below the upstairs bath on page 22. Is there any history there?"

## HOA Documents

- **Dues:** the amount, how often they're due, and what they cover (water, sewer, trash, exterior insurance, snow removal, landscaping)
- **Special assessments:** current, approved, or discussed in the minutes
- **Reserves:** the date of the reserve study, recommended versus actual contributions, and the reserve balance. An old study or underfunded reserves are a question to ask, not a verdict.
- **Budget:** operating deficits, delinquency levels if shown, and large insurance increases
- **Minutes:** upcoming projects (roofs, siding, asphalt, amenities), litigation, insurance claims, assessment discussions, and pending rule changes
- **Leasing:** rental restrictions, minimum lease terms, short-term rental bans, rental caps or waitlists. This is critical if the buyer plans to rent.
- **Use restrictions:** parking, RVs and trailers, pets, exterior changes, home businesses
- **Insurance (condos and townhomes):** what the master policy covers versus what the owner must insure. Send this to the buyer's insurance agent for an accurate quote.
- **Fees at closing:** reinvestment and transfer fees. Show the reinvestment fee as a percentage of the purchase price, and see `utah-repc.md` for the cap.
- **Litigation:** any that's pending. Flag it for the buyer's lender too, because pending litigation can affect loan eligibility, especially for condos.

## Title Commitment

Scan the requirements and exceptions for:
- Liens, judgments, or notices of pending litigation
- Easements that cross the usable part of the lot
- Encroachments or survey matters
- Unpaid assessments

Flag anything that looks unusual for the title officer. Don't interpret legal effect.

## Fair Housing Reminder

Review documents and property conditions only. Never describe who lives in the community, the area's demographics, school quality, or safety, even when a document or the agent mentions them.
