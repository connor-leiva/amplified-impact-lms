# Seller Objections

Last verified: 2026-11-08. The only state or industry facts here point to `utah-pricing-context.md` (Utah) and `comp-method.md` (every state), which carry their own sources.

Pick the four to six most likely objections for this seller, based on what the agent shared and what the data shows. For each, write a short answer the agent can say out loud, built on numbers from this CMA. The patterns below are frameworks, not scripts. Never present a response without real numbers in it.

Every answer follows the same shape: acknowledge what's real in the objection, show the data, and tie it back to the seller's goal.

## "Zillow says it's worth more."

What's behind it: an outside number the seller trusts because it's neutral and free.

Pattern: an online estimate is a model's guess; the CMA shows what buyers actually paid for the closest homes. Put the estimate next to the closest solds' prices. The buyer's appraiser will use closed sales like these, not the estimate.
- In Utah: sale prices aren't public record, so estimates work without the closed prices the MLS holds (see `utah-pricing-context.md`).
- Outside Utah (general mode): don't say whether the state discloses sale prices. Stick to "these are the actual closed sales."

Don't: mock the site or claim it's always wrong.

## "My neighbor's house sold for X."

What's behind it: secondhand information, often wrong.

Pattern: check that sale in the CMA before the conversation. If it's there, show its real price, any concessions, and how it differs from this home (above-grade size, basement finish, lot, updates). If it isn't in the CMA, say plainly that the agent can't verify that number yet and will look it up in the MLS.

Don't: share private details about the neighbor's sale beyond what the MLS shows clients.

## "But we have more square feet."

What's behind it: a total-square-foot number from a listing site or tax record that includes the basement.

Pattern: split it. Show above-grade and basement square footage for the subject and the closest solds, and the price per above-grade square foot. Acknowledge the basement adds value, then show what this comp set does and doesn't prove about how much (see `comp-method.md`).

Don't: tell the seller the basement doesn't count.

## "Let's start high. We can always come down later."

What's behind it: fear of leaving money on the table.

Pattern: use this comp set's own history. Compare the days on market and the sale-to-original-list ratio for homes that sold at their first price against homes that reduced, and point to any expired listing that started high. If this comp set shows reduced homes taking longer or selling further below their original price, say so with the numbers. If it doesn't, don't make the argument.

Don't: predict a specific outcome for this home.

## "We need to net X for our next home."

What's behind it: a real financial need, often the actual reason for the number.

Pattern: separate need from value with care. Buyers and appraisers don't see the seller's next purchase, only the comps. Then help with the need: the agent can ask the title or escrow company for a seller net sheet at the recommended price, and options might include timing, a contingency on the next purchase, or a talk with a lender. Suggest a lender conversation without giving loan advice.

Don't: do commission or payoff math, dismiss the need, or put the seller's financial details in anything client-facing.

## "We put $X into upgrades."

What's behind it: real money and real pride.

Pattern: acknowledge the work, then show how updated comps in this set sold against less-updated ones. Some upgrades show up in price, and some mainly help the home sell faster. Let the comps show which is which.

Don't: tell the seller their upgrades were a waste, or put a dollar value on them the comps don't show.

## "Another agent said they could get more."

What's behind it: the seller wants to be sure they're not leaving money on the table.

Pattern: don't attack the other number. Walk through the closed sales and invite the seller to ask the other agent which sales support their price. The agent's number is the one the comps defend at appraisal.

Don't: criticize the other agent or speculate about their motives.

## Price Reduction: "It's only been a few weeks." / "The right buyer hasn't come yet."

What's behind it: hope, and fatigue with the process.

Pattern: show the subject's days on market against this comp set's median for solds, plus what has sold or gone pending nearby since the listing went live, and at what prices. If there were showings without offers, the market has been looking and passing. Recommend a specific new price tied to the newest comps and the price bands.

Don't: blame the seller, the home, or the photos unless the agent's feedback shows a specific fixable issue.

## Price Reduction: "Let's wait for spring." / "Let's wait for rates to drop."

What's behind it: hoping the market will come to the price.

Pattern: present what waiting means that the seller can see: days on market that buyers will see, new competing listings in this comp set, and carrying the home longer. Present the choice clearly: adjust now, take the listing off and relist later, or hold and accept the trade-offs. The seller decides.

Don't: forecast rates or seasonal prices.

## Price Reduction: "Can't we offer a credit instead of cutting the price?"

What's behind it: a credit feels smaller than a cut.

Pattern: a credit can help a buyer with cash to close, but it doesn't change the price buyers see in their search or the price they compare. Loan programs cap seller concessions (see `comp-method.md`), so any number goes through the buyer's lender. If this comp set shows concessions on several solds, say so.

Don't: promise a credit will produce an offer.
