# Response Guide

Last verified: 2026-11-08

How to read a buyer's repair request from the seller's side, sort each item, and weigh the options. The four categories line up with the tiers a buyer's agent uses for the same report (Tier 1 Safety, Tier 2 Major System, Tier 3 Maintenance, Tier 4 Cosmetic and informational), so both sides can talk about an item in the same terms.

## Match the Request to the Report

For each requested item, find the finding it rests on and record:
- the page and item number, the inspector's words (quote where the wording matters), and the inspector's rating if the report uses one
- the inspector's recommendation, in the inspector's words: "recommend evaluation by a licensed roofing contractor," "budget for replacement," "monitor," "no action needed"

Then check each item:
- **Page reference.** If the request cites the wrong page, give the right one.
- **Ask versus recommendation.** The inspector's recommendation is the fair anchor for a counter. Common gaps: replace when the inspector said repair or service; fix when the inspector said evaluate; "bring to current code" when the inspector called it an upgrade or said it may not have been required when the home was built.
- **No finding.** The report doesn't mention the condition, or says the system wasn't tested or inspected (a winterized sprinkler system, a shut-off appliance). Ask the buyer's agent where the condition is documented before agreeing to anything.
- **Age only.** The inspector notes age or remaining life but reports the system working with no defect. Age is not a defect.
- **Further evaluation recommended.** Offer a two-step answer: the seller hires a licensed specialist to evaluate and completes the repairs the specialist says are needed for safe operation. The other route is the buyer getting the evaluation before the Due Diligence Deadline.
- **Vague asks.** "Fix all electrical issues" becomes the specific findings the report lists, nothing more.
- **Specialty reports** (radon, sewer scope, roof, structural): same checks. For radon, read the actual result and the test dates; the EPA action level is 4 pCi/L (see `utah-repair-response.md` in Utah).

Then read the rest of the report. Findings the buyer did not request stay out of the response. They belong in the disclosure note, because the seller now knows them.

## Categories

**Safety.** Conditions that could hurt someone: gas leaks or carbon monoxide risk (cracked heat exchanger, improper venting), wiring the inspector calls a safety concern (double-tapped breakers, exposed conductors), missing smoke or CO alarms, missing stair or deck railings, a garage door that doesn't auto-reverse, a missing water heater relief discharge pipe, a dryer duct the inspector calls a fire hazard, radon at or above 4 pCi/L, active water intrusion with suspected mold.

**Major System.** The roof, foundation, drainage toward the foundation, sewer lateral defects, a furnace, air conditioner or water heater with a reported defect, electrical capacity, plumbing supply problems, failure of the building envelope. A system that is only old goes here as "age only," not as a defect.

**Maintenance.** Routine repairs and service: a dripping faucet, caulking, a dirty filter, routine HVAC service, minor grading, a loose fixture.

**Cosmetic.** Wear and finishes: stains, worn carpet, paint, settlement cracks the inspector calls typical, fogged window panes the inspector calls cosmetic.

Keep the inspector's own rating. If you place an item in a different category, say why in one line.

## Disclosed or Visible

**Disclosed.** Find the item in the seller's property condition disclosure. Record the page, the question, the seller's words and the date the seller signed. Then compare dates:
- Signed before the Offer Reference Date, with the buyer's acknowledgment dated before it too: the buyer had it when writing the offer. This is the strongest basis for pushing back.
- Delivered after Acceptance, by the Seller Disclosure Deadline: the buyer learned it during due diligence, which is what due diligence is for. It was disclosed, but "you priced it into your offer" doesn't hold.
- Dates unclear or missing: say so and ask the agent.
- Partly disclosed: the disclosure names one condition and the report finds more (roots disclosed; an offset and a belly found by the sewer scope). Split the item into the disclosed part and the new part, and read each separately.

Disclosure doesn't take away the buyer's right to cancel by the Due Diligence Deadline.

If the disclosure says "no known" issue and the report shows evidence of one, record both as facts for the agent. Sellers often don't know. Never suggest the seller knew, and never accuse anyone.

**Visible at showings.** Use only the documents or the agent: the report describes a surface condition in plain view in a lived-in room (a stained carpet, a cracked driveway, fogged panes), the listing remarks mention it, or the agent says so. Otherwise write "agent to confirm." A condition in plain view was part of what the buyer saw when making the offer.

## The Reasonableness Read

- **Reasonable:** Safety items. Major System defects the report documents, when the ask matches the inspector's recommendation.
- **Negotiable:** Major System items where the ask goes past the recommendation. Partly disclosed items. Items disclosed only during due diligence. Maintenance items with a real defect behind them. "Further evaluation" items.
- **Push back:** Cosmetic items. Conditions visible at showings. Items disclosed before the offer. Age-only items. Requests with no matching finding. Upgrades the report doesn't call defects.

Safety outranks the other rules. A disclosed or visible safety item is still usually worth fixing for certainty, because the buyer can cancel by the Due Diligence Deadline and the next buyer's inspector will find it too. Say so when it applies.

## Response Options and Tradeoffs

| Option | Fits when | The seller gains | The seller risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Repair by a licensed contractor, paid invoices before Settlement | Safety items, work where quality matters, enough time before Settlement | Chooses the contractor; cost set by the bid; a documented repair to show any future buyer | Scheduling before Settlement; a walk-through dispute over the work; costs past the bid |
| Evaluate, then repair what's needed | "Further evaluation recommended" items | Scope limited to what a licensed specialist finds | Cost unknown until the evaluation; takes time |
| Credit toward the buyer's closing costs | Short timelines; the buyer wants control of the work | No work, no walk-through dispute, simpler closing | Dollar for dollar off the net; the buyer's lender caps seller contributions and confirms the amount |
| Price reduction | Large items the buyer plans to handle after closing | Simple, one number | Changes the loan amount, so the lender must be updated; can affect the appraisal and loan review |
| Home warranty | Age-only systems that work today | Answers the worry without replacing a working system | Costs what the quote says; coverage has limits; it isn't a repair |
| Decline | Cosmetic, visible, disclosed before the offer, age only, no finding | No cost | The buyer can cancel by the Due Diligence Deadline and the earnest money goes back to the buyer |
| Partial or split | A real item with a large ask | Shares the cost from a bid | Show the arithmetic from the bid; the buyer may still counter |

## Strategy by Seller Priority

- **Net:** agree to Safety items, by repair through the seller's own bid so the seller controls the cost. Decline Cosmetic, age-only, visible and no-finding items. Offer a credit only where a bid sets the amount. Total the package.
- **Certainty:** agree to Safety items and documented Major System defects. Consider a credit for the Negotiable items so nothing hangs on a contractor's schedule. Answer quickly. Say plainly that this costs more.
- **Timing:** prefer credits to repairs whenever a bid's earliest start or lead time can't finish before the completion date the response sets (often five days before the Settlement Deadline). Keep the response short enough to sign well before the Due Diligence Deadline.

For each package, list the items, the remedy, the bid total and the main risk. Don't predict how the buyer will respond. Use market context (a backup offer, days on market, other interest) only if the agent mentioned it.

## Reading a Contractor Bid

- **Who:** the company, the license number shown on the bid (in Utah, verify it at DOPL; see `utah-repair-response.md`), and the date
- **Scope:** does it match the inspector's finding and the buyer's ask? Is it itemized?
- **Price and options:** every option with its amount
- **Exclusions and conditions:** permits, hidden damage, change orders, weather
- **Valid until:** against when the seller will decide
- **Earliest start or lead time:** against the Settlement Deadline and the completion date in the response
- **Warranty** on the work
- **Payment terms:** say a deposit is required if it is. Never repeat bank, account, routing or wire details. If a bid asks for payment by wire, add: "Verify payment instructions by phone with the contractor at a number you already have."

## Net Impact Math

- Package total: the sum of bid lines for the items the seller would fund, shown as arithmetic ("$1,450 + $2,380 = $3,830").
- Credit or price reduction: the amount offered, taken from a bid or a stated share of a bid, with the arithmetic shown.
- Existing seller contributions in the contract: if a new credit goes on the same line, show the new total.
- Items without a bid: "needs bid," left out of the total and counted ("2 items still need bids").
- No commission, title or proration math unless a seller net sheet is uploaded. If one is, use only its figures.
