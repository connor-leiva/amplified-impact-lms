# Offer Terms: Reading the Listing, Choosing Each Term, Showing the Risk

Last verified: 2026-11-08. Sources are listed at the end. These rules apply in every state; Utah section numbers and form details live in `utah-offer-terms.md`.

The buyer decides every term. This file helps the agent explain each choice: what it does, what a more competitive version gives the seller, and what it risks for the buyer.

## 1. Reading the Listing's Position

Use only what the MLS sheet, the comp file and the agent's message show. Never guess at other offers, the seller's reasons or the listing agent's plans.

| Signal | Where it comes from | Points toward |
|---|---|---|
| Days on market against the comp set's median for solds | MLS sheet; comp file | Well under the median: competition is likely. Well over: the buyer has room. |
| Cumulative days on market, relist or DOM reset | MLS sheet or its history report | A reset hides time on market; use cumulative days when shown. |
| Price changes (how many, how much, how recent) | Price history | Two or more cuts: the seller has already moved toward the market. |
| List price against the supported range | Comp math | At or above the top: appraisal risk for any financed buyer. Inside or below: room to compete on price. |
| Status history (back on market, expired, withdrawn) | History report | Ask the listing agent why; don't guess. |
| Offer deadline, "highest and best," multiple offers | The agent's message only | A competitive situation, as reported by the listing agent. |
| Seller-paid items in public remarks (closing costs, rate buydown, home warranty) | Public remarks | The seller has offered money; a concession ask fits. |
| As-is, vacant, estate, relocation, new construction in progress | Public remarks; agent-only remarks | Possession and settlement flexibility may matter more to the seller than price. Use agent-only remarks for strategy only. |

Call the position **Competitive**, **Balanced** or **Buyer Leverage**, with the two or three signals behind it. Say "the listing agent said" for anything that comes only from the agent's message.

## 2. Reading the Preapproval Letter

Pull: the lender's name, the letter date and any expiration, the loan program (conventional, FHA, VA, USDA or cash with proof of funds), the maximum price or loan amount, the down payment if stated, what the lender verified (income, assets, credit) and the conditions listed (appraisal, title, sale of a home, final underwriting).

- Lenders use "prequalification" and "preapproval" differently; the CFPB says both letters describe how much a lender is generally willing to lend "based on certain assumptions," and neither is "a guaranteed loan offer." Report what the letter says it verified; don't grade the lender.
- Flag: a letter that expires before the recommended Settlement Deadline; a program that doesn't match the offer; a recommended price above the letter's maximum; a condition such as the sale of the buyer's home that the offer's terms must match.
- Never repeat loan numbers, account numbers, credit scores, income or account balances. If the letter states the cash left after closing (reserves), use it only in the agent-facing risk ledger and say it comes from the letter.
- Never put the buyer's maximum price, cash on hand or reasons for moving in anything meant for the listing side.

## 3. Choosing Each Term

For each term: the protective version (keeps the buyer's options), the competitive version (gives the seller more certainty), and how to pick.

**Price.** Built from the closest solds (see `comp-method.md`). In a Competitive position, an offer above the top of the supported range is the buyer's choice, and the risk ledger shows the gap in dollars. In Buyer Leverage, the supported range, the price cuts and the days on market justify an offer below list. Never recommend above the buyer's stated maximum or the preapproval letter's maximum.

**Earnest money.** A larger deposit signals commitment. It is at risk only as the contract allows (see the Utah file for when). Use an amount the agent, the buyer or the listing agent named. If none was given, write [earnest money amount] and explain the trade; don't pick a "typical" amount.

**Due diligence (or inspection) period.** Shorter is more attractive to a seller and leaves less time to inspect, get specialist follow-ups, review HOA documents and negotiate. Build it from what has to fit: the date the seller's disclosures are due, the inspector's availability, likely specialist follow-ups for the home's age and features, and HOA review. Suggest the agent confirm the inspector's availability before shortening it.

**Appraisal and financing deadlines.** Take the days from the lender. Shorter gives the seller certainty sooner; too short risks a deadline passing before the appraisal or loan approval is in, which can put the earnest money at risk. When the lender's needs aren't in the inputs, write [days from lender] and put "Ask the lender" in the questions.

**Settlement and possession.** Often the cheapest way to compete: match the seller's preferred date and possession needs when the listing agent shares them. A rent-back needs a separate written agreement. Settlement must leave the lender enough time after the financing deadline.

**Appraisal protection.** Three versions, from most protective to most competitive:
1. Keep the appraisal condition. If the appraisal comes in under the price, the buyer can cancel or renegotiate as the contract allows.
2. Keep it, plus a written commitment to cover a gap up to a set dollar amount in cash. The amount must be cash the lender confirms the buyer has beyond the down payment and closing costs.
3. Waive it. The buyer must bring the full difference between the appraised value and the price in cash, or risk the deposit.
Show the possible gap in dollars: offer price minus the highest closed sale that supports the range, labeled "a reference point, not a prediction of the appraisal." For a conventional purchase loan, Fannie Mae measures loan-to-value against "the lower of the sales price or the current appraised value," so a low appraisal raises the cash the buyer needs. The lender confirms the exact figure. Never recommend version 3 without the lender confirming the cash in writing, and never present it as the default way to win.

**FHA and VA.** The lender's required amendatory clause (in Utah, the FHA/VA Loan Addendum) lets the buyer walk away without losing the deposit if the price exceeds the FHA appraised value or the VA reasonable value, and lets the buyer proceed anyway. Waiving a contract's appraisal condition doesn't remove that right. Strength comes from a gap commitment the lender confirms, a realistic price, the repair amount the seller is asked to cover, and clean dates.

**Financing protection.** Keep it on any financed offer. A cash offer needs proof of funds. Waiving financing protection on a financed purchase puts the deposit at risk if the loan fails; state that risk and say the lender must confirm the buyer's position before the buyer decides.

**Seller concessions.** A concession lowers the buyer's cash at closing; the seller looks at price minus concessions. Show both: the offer price, the concession, and the price after concessions. Under Fannie Mae's rules for conventional loans, seller-paid financing concessions are capped by loan-to-value; any amount above the buyer's closing costs (or above the cap) is treated as a sales concession and deducted from the price; and seller money can't be used for the down payment or to meet reserve requirements. FHA, VA and USDA set their own rules. The lender confirms the amount for this buyer, so take the number from the lender's estimate of closing costs or write [concession amount from lender]. Appraisers adjust comparable sales for concessions, and the property still has to appraise at the contract price.

**Sale of the buyer's home.** A real protection for a buyer who needs that sale, and a real weakness in a Competitive position. Never drop it to win if the buyer needs it. Lay out the trade and send loan alternatives (such as bridge financing) to the lender.

**Escalation.** Three numbers from the buyer: the starting price, the increment and the cap. The cap must be at or below the buyer's stated maximum and the preapproval maximum, and every price up to the cap faces the same appraisal question. The listing side learns the buyer's cap if it is reached. The language comes from the brokerage's approved escalation addendum, reviewed by the broker; never draft it.

**Lead-based paint (homes built before 1978).** Federal rules give the buyer a 10-day opportunity for a lead-based paint risk assessment or inspection, unless the parties agree in writing to a different period or the buyer waives it in writing. Shortening or waiving it is the buyer's call; say what the buyer gives up.

**Home warranty, inclusions, Seller's Compensation Contribution.** Asking the seller to pay for a warranty or the buyer's brokerage lowers the seller's net, the same as a concession. Take any compensation amount only from the agent's message or the buyer agreement; never recommend a fee (see `buyer-agreements.md`).

**Offer deadline.** Match the listing agent's instructions when given. Otherwise long enough for the seller to respond and short enough to keep the offer from being shopped; the agent's call.

## 4. The Risk Ledger

One row for each term that is more competitive than the protective version:

| Term | What it gives the seller | What it risks for the buyer | Who confirms |
|---|---|---|---|
| Example: price above the closest solds | A higher number | If the appraisal comes in at the top closed sale ($X), the buyer needs $Y more in cash or uses the appraisal condition | Lender |

Plain English, dollar figures from the inputs, math shown. Name who confirms each risk (lender, inspector, broker, title).

## 5. Questions for the Listing Agent

Pick the five to eight that matter for this listing:
- When and how offers will be reviewed, and whether a highest-and-best round is planned
- The seller's preferred Settlement date and possession timing, and whether a rent-back is needed
- Whether the seller would consider a contribution toward the buyer's closing costs (or confirm the one in the remarks)
- Which purchase contract version the listing side is working on (in Utah, during the overlap)
- Whether any inspections, HOA documents, water share or well records, or disclosures are available before writing
- Why the listing came back on the market or changed status, if the history shows it
- Where earnest money should be deposited

Never ask about the seller's personal circumstances beyond timing, and never volunteer the buyer's maximum, cash or reasons.

## 6. Buyer Letters and Photos

NAR's senior counsel has warned that buyer "love letters" raise fair housing issues "because they often contain personal information about the buyer, such as their race and culture or traditions," and the same NAR article says "Real estate professionals should avoid helping their buyers draft them." Advise against a letter or photos with the offer. Never draft one, never summarize one the agent mentions, and never use what it says about the buyer anywhere in the output.
