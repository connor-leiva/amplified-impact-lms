# Utah Property Notes

Last verified: 2026-11-08

These are Utah facts that come up when a buyer looks at a home: water, HOA rental rules, HOA transfer fees, radon, recording calls, public infrastructure districts, and new construction. This is not legal advice. The signed contract, the recorded documents, and the current statute control. When an item says "confirm with" someone, the skill says so in its output and does not state the item as fact.

## Two REPC Versions Are in Use

The Utah Real Estate Commission approved a new Real Estate Purchase Contract (REPC). It took effect November 1, 2026. As of January 1, 2027, it replaces the version marked effective December 4, 2024. Until then, either version may appear. Section numbers differ between them, so read section numbers from the uploaded contract. This file gives both where it cites the REPC.

## Water Rights and Water Shares

### The Terms

- A water right is real property in Utah. Water rights are bought and sold much like real estate.
- A water share is stock in a water company, such as an irrigation, canal, or ditch company. Shares are generally not transferred by deed. Each company has its own transfer steps. The most common: the seller endorses and delivers the stock certificate, and the buyer presents it to the company for a new certificate. Each company sets how much water a share delivers and what fees and assessments it charges.
- Culinary water is the indoor domestic supply. The state addendum notes call domestic use "indoor culinary" use.
- Secondary water is water that is not culinary and is delivered for irrigating landscaping or a garden. A secondary water supplier is an entity that supplies pressurized secondary water (Utah Code 73-10-34). Listings may call it pressurized irrigation or PI. Confirm the provider.
- Utah law requires secondary water suppliers to meter pressurized secondary water for each user by January 1, 2030, with some exemptions (Utah Code 73-10-34).
- Water rights not used for seven years are subject to forfeiture. A nonuse application can protect a right (Division of Water Rights FAQ, citing Utah Code 73-1-4).
- The county recorder is the office of record for title. The Division of Water Rights ownership record is not the official title record.

### The Water Rights Addendum

- Utah Code 57-3-109 defines a water rights addendum as an addendum to a deed, in a form approved by the Legislature, that either identifies the water rights transferred or states that none are transferred.
- A person recording a deed may submit the addendum with it. The grantor (seller) completes and signs it. The grantee (buyer) signs to acknowledge receipt. The county recorder sends a copy to the state engineer.
- The Division of Water Rights publishes two versions: the Water Rights Addendum to Land Deeds and the Water Rights Addendum to Water Deeds.
- On the land deed addendum, the seller checks one box: all water rights used on the parcel are conveyed, only a portion is conveyed, no water rights are conveyed, or water rights are conveyed by a separate deed.
- Section A or B lists water right numbers. A partial conveyance states the portion in acre-feet and the uses (families, irrigated acres, livestock units).
- Section C holds the seller's disclosures: water company shares being endorsed and delivered (how many, which company), who provides culinary water, who provides outdoor water, that no water service is available, or other water facts.
- The form's notes say buying a water right does not guarantee the right is in good standing, that the seller has clear title, that the Division will recognize the ownership change, or that it will approve future changes. They encourage due diligence.
- If the Division does not update ownership from the recorded deed and addendum, the owner must file a Report of Water Right Conveyance (Utah Code 73-1-10(3)). The buyer must keep a current address on file with the Division.
- Not every appurtenant water right has a water right number.

### What the REPC Says

- The purchase price includes the water rights and water shares, if any, that are the legal source of the seller's current water service. They transfer at closing by deed or other legal instrument.
- REPC effective November 1, 2026: Section 7.5 covers culinary or domestic, irrigation, secondary, and stock watering service. Seller Disclosures include evidence of the water rights or shares (Section 11(i)).
- REPC effective December 4, 2024: Section 1.4 covers culinary and irrigation service and has a line for excluded water rights or shares. Seller Disclosures include the same evidence (Section 7(i)).
- Both versions list water sources, availability, and quality as due diligence items.

### What Buyers Should Ask

- Which water rights or shares come with the home: water right numbers, or the share count and company name.
- Who provides culinary water, and who provides outdoor or secondary water. Whether the secondary connection is metered and how it is billed.
- For shares: the company's transfer steps, transfer fees, annual assessments, and how much water a share delivers. Ask the company.
- For water rights: the status in the Division's records and any evidence of recent use.
- Whether the seller will sign the Water Rights Addendum at closing, and which box.
- Confirm with the title company, the water company, and the Division of Water Rights. For complex rights, the addendum notes point to attorneys, engineers, surveyors, and title professionals who work with water rights.

## HOA Rental Restrictions

Two statutes say almost the same thing. Utah Code 57-8a-209 covers community associations (lots). Utah Code 57-8-10.1 covers condominiums (units).

### What an Association May Do

- Restrict the number and term of rentals, or prohibit rentals.
- The restriction must be in the recorded declaration (CC&Rs) or an amendment to it. One exception: a rule may set a minimum lease term of six months or less.
- Require the owner and renter to sign an association lease addendum.
- If at least 35% of units may be rentals, charge a rental owner up to $200 once every 12 months for added administrative costs. Notice, a board meeting, and a majority board vote come first.

### What an Association May Not Require

Except in narrow cases, an association may not require a rental owner to:
- get approval of a renter,
- hand over a rental application, credit report, background check, or proof of the renter's age,
- pay an extra assessment, fine, or fee because the unit is a rental (other than the $200 fee above),
- use the association's lease, or get the association's approval of a lease.

The narrow cases are a court order or discovery, and an association whose declaration lawfully restricts occupancy by a certain class of individuals, when it uses the information to check compliance.

For community associations only: an association may not restrict or prohibit renting an internal accessory dwelling unit that meets land use, building, health, and fire codes (57-8a-209(10)). The condominium section has no matching provision.

### Required Exemptions

An association that restricts rentals or charges the rental fee must exempt:
- an owner in the military, during deployment,
- a unit occupied by the owner's parent, child, or sibling,
- an owner relocated by an employer for two years or less,
- a unit owned by an entity and occupied by a person with voting rights and at least 25% ownership,
- a unit owned by an estate planning trust for the current resident or the resident's parent, child, or sibling.

### Grandfathering

- An owner who was renting before the restriction took effect may keep renting, without the rental fee. For condominiums, the cutoff is when the restriction is recorded with the county recorder.
- That right ends when the owner moves in, when an owner or officer of an owning entity or trust moves in, or when the unit is transferred.
- A transfer is a deed conveyance, a grant of a life estate, or, for an entity owner, a transfer of more than 75% of the ownership interests in 12 months.
- A buyer does not inherit the seller's grandfathered rental status, because the sale is a transfer.

### Exceptions That Change the Answer

- A declaration recorded before the developer sold the first lot or unit may restrict rentals without the exemptions and grandfathering above. This matters in new communities.
- Unanimous approval by all owners allows a restriction without the exemptions.
- Timeshares are excluded.
- Older associations: parts of these sections do not apply to an association formed before May 12, 2009 (for condos, an initial declaration recorded before that date), unless it adopted or amended a rental restriction on or after May 12, 2015. Confirm with the HOA manager or a real estate attorney.
- These sections do not limit age requirements for housing for older persons.

### What to Ask For

- The recorded CC&Rs and every amendment with a rental restriction, with recording dates. Bylaws, rules, and fee and fine schedules. The November 2026 REPC lists the CC&Rs, bylaws, fee and fine schedules, and rules as Seller Disclosures (Section 11(d)). The December 2024 REPC lists the CC&Rs and rules (Section 7(d)).
- HOA minutes, budget, and financial statement. The November 2026 REPC adds the reserve analysis (Section 11(e)). The December 2024 REPC lists minutes, budget, and financial statement (Section 7(e)).
- The current rental count against any cap, any waitlist, any minimum lease term, any rental fee, and any required lease addendum. Ask the HOA manager.
- Whether the seller is renting under grandfathering, and a reminder that it ends at the sale.
- How the rules apply to a specific plan: confirm with a real estate attorney.

## HOA Reinvestment Fees (Utah Code 57-1-46)

A reinvestment fee is a fee a common interest association charges a buyer or seller because the property transfers. It is dedicated to benefiting the common areas or the burdened property.

### The Cap After H.B. 306 (2026)

- 2026 H.B. 306, Reinvestment Fee Amendments, took effect May 6, 2026.
- For a reinvestment fee covenant recorded on or after May 6, 2026, the fee may not exceed 0.5% of the property's value. In a low-amenity association it may not exceed 0.25%.
- A low-amenity association has only detached single-family homes and does not provide capital-intensive infrastructure maintenance to members.
- Property in a large master planned development is not subject to the cap. That is an approved development of at least 500 acres or 500 units with a commitment to fund or maintain items such as common infrastructure, amenities, or open space.
- Before H.B. 306, this subsection capped fees at 0.5% for covenants recorded on or after March 16, 2010, with the same master planned development exception. H.B. 306 changed the recording date in the cap to May 6, 2026. How the cap applies today to a covenant recorded between 2010 and May 6, 2026: confirm with the title company or a real estate attorney.

### Other Rules in the Statute

- Since May 6, 2026, an association may not impose a reinvestment fee unless the declaration or a reinvestment fee covenant authorizes it, a majority of voting interests approves it (or a higher share if the documents require), and, outside a large master planned development, at least 50% of the fee goes into reserve funds.
- The association sets the amount under its documents, after notice. Members can remove or change the fee at a special meeting where at least 51% of voting interests attend and vote.
- A reinvestment fee covenant recorded on or after March 16, 2010 is void unless a separate notice of the covenant is recorded in the county.
- For a covenant recorded on or after March 16, 2010, the fee cannot be enforced on an involuntary transfer, a transfer by court order, a bona fide transfer to a family member within three degrees of blood relation (with proof), a transfer at death, or a transfer by a financial institution (except the association's direct transfer costs, up to $250).
- A transfer fee covenant recorded on or after March 16, 2010 is void.
- An administrative setup fee imposed on or after May 7, 2025 is void unless the association uses it only for costs of the transfer.
- H.B. 306 requires an association registering with the Department of Commerce to state whether it charges a reinvestment or transfer fee. The HOA Ombudsman's website must say that no one may rely on that registry disclosure when preparing purchase documents. Get the fee from the HOA and the title company.

### In the REPC

- The parties choose who pays "change of ownership fees" (transfer fees, community enhancement fees, HOA reinvestment fees) by checkbox: Section 8.3(a) in the November 2026 REPC, Section 4.3(c) in the December 2024 REPC.
- The November 2026 REPC adds that HOA buyer setup or administrative fees are the buyer's and are not change of ownership fees.
- If a quoted fee looks above the cap that applies, flag it for the title company to confirm. Do not call it unlawful.

## Radon

- Utah DEQ states that 1 in 3 homes in Utah have elevated radon levels, and that radon is the number two cause of lung cancer. Its mitigation page calls radon the leading cause of lung cancer in nonsmokers.
- EPA action level: EPA recommends fixing a home at 4 pCi/L (picocuries per liter) or more. EPA also says to consider fixing between 2 and 4 pCi/L.
- Utah DEQ: if a result is between 4 and 6 pCi/L, consider a long-term test. If below 4, retest every two to five years.
- Testing steps on DEQ's page (from EPA): take a short-term test. At 4 pCi/L or higher, follow up with a long-term test or a second short-term test. If the first result is more than twice 4 pCi/L, take the second short-term test right away. Fix the home if the long-term result, or the average of the two short-term results, is 4 pCi/L or more.
- Mitigation: hire a certified radon mitigation contractor (DEQ links the NRPP and NRSB directories). DEQ suggests getting bids and a signed contract that guarantees a level at or below 4 pCi/L. It says most homes can be brought to 2 pCi/L or below.
- After installation: DEQ says to test after the system has run 24 hours, check the system's manometer, and test every two years even with a system. EPA says to test again after a fix, and to retest if you start living on a lower level such as a basement.
- For buyers, EPA says to know the radon level of any home you consider, ask the seller for test results, ask for information on any reduction system, and test if the home has not been tested.
- DEQ publishes median radon levels by ZIP code. A ZIP median does not tell you a specific home's level.
- Costs: do not quote a mitigation cost. Use the contractor's bid.

## Recording a Conversation

- Utah is a one-party consent state. Under Utah Code 77-23a-4(7)(b), a person may record a wire, electronic, or oral communication if that person is a party to it or one party has given prior consent, unless the purpose is a criminal or tortious act.
- Some other states require every party's consent (for example, Washington, RCW 9.73.030). When anyone on the call is in another state, confirm with the broker or an attorney. Asking everyone before recording avoids the question.

## Public Infrastructure Districts (Utah Code Title 17D, Chapter 4)

### What They Are

- A public infrastructure district (PID) is a special district created under the Public Infrastructure District Act. It is a political subdivision of the state, separate from the city or county.
- A creating entity (a county, municipality, basic special district, or development authority) approves the district's governing document.
- Creation requires a petition signed by 100% of the surface property owners in the area. A development authority may create one with the written consent of all surface owners.
- A board runs it. The governing document may move board seats from appointment to election based on milestones it sets.
- The governing document sets the boundaries, the property tax levy rate limit, the debt limit, and the infrastructure the district will build. Raising the levy rate limit needs the consent of 100% of surface property owners.
- Since May 6, 2026, the board must dissolve the district after its debts are paid, its obligations are met, and its improvements are completed, accepted, and transferred. A district created by a development authority that provides ongoing services is excepted (17D-4-401).

### How They Are Paid

- A PID issues bonds. A PID bond must mature within 40 years of issuance.
- The bonds are repaid by a property tax levy on property inside the district. For all purposes, including limited tax bonds, the levy may not exceed 0.015 per dollar of taxable value. General obligation bonds and a few listed cases are outside that limit (17D-4-303).
- A PID may also charge fees set by a fee schedule (17D-4-302).
- On nonpayment of a PID tax, fee, or charge, the district may add a property tax penalty at an annual rate of 0.07, on top of other lawful penalties (17D-4-304).

### Disclosure Rules for Buyers

- Recorded notice: within 30 days after the Lieutenant Governor certifies the district, the board must record a notice with the county recorder. It gives the boundaries, says the governing document is on file with the creating entity, says the district may levy a property tax to finance and repay infrastructure, and states the maximum rate. The district may not levy a tax or collect a fee until that notice is recorded (17D-4-303(3) and (4)).
- Seller disclosure: since May 6, 2026, in a sale of residential property inside a PID, the seller or the seller's representative must make sure the expected annual cost of the district's final tax rate, from the last equalized assessment rolls, is in a disclosure document at or before closing (Utah Code 57-1-49, enacted by 2026 H.B. 507).
- In the REPC: per the Real Estate Commission's September 23, 2026 minutes, the PID reference was removed from the special assessment line (Section 8.2) of the November 2026 REPC, so a PID levy is not treated as a special assessment there. UAR's counsel said the levy is better handled as a proration under Section 8.1.
- "At or before closing" is late for a buyer's decision. Ask during due diligence.

### What to Ask

- Is the property inside a PID? Check the title commitment for a recorded notice.
- The governing document (from the creating entity), the maximum levy rate, and the current annual amount on the tax bill (from the county).
- Confirm with the title company and the county treasurer or assessor.

## New Construction: Utah Rules Confirmed

- PIDs: the recorded notice and the 57-1-49 seller disclosure above apply to new homes inside a district.
- Rental restrictions: a declaration recorded before the developer's first sale may restrict rentals without the statutory exemptions (57-8a-209(5), 57-8-10.1(5)).
- Contract form: the November 2026 REPC says Utah law normally requires licensees to use it, but a licensee may use forms prepared by a party's legal counsel instead. A builder's purchase agreement is not the REPC. Read its own terms, and send contract questions to a real estate attorney.
- Land sales: Utah's Uniform Land Sales Practices Act (57-11) does not apply to a unit with a residential building on it, or one the seller is legally obligated to finish building within two years (57-11-4(1)(b)). For a vacant lot with no build obligation, confirm with the Division of Real Estate's land sales program.
- Radon: EPA notes that radon-reducing features can be built into a new home. Ask the builder what is included. Whether the city or county requires them: confirm with the local building department.
- Water: ask the builder who provides culinary and secondary water, and whether any water rights or shares transfer.
- Warranties, liens, and other builder disclosures: no other Utah-specific new-construction disclosure statute was confirmed for this file. Confirm with a real estate attorney and the title company.
