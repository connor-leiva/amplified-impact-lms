# Utah New Construction and the REPC

Utah facts for reviewing a builder's purchase agreement. Use only for a Utah property; outside Utah, skip this file. Parts: Utah New Construction Notes; the November 1, 2026 REPC; the December 4, 2024 REPC (section map and new construction); Licensee Forms; Utah Property Notes (public infrastructure districts, HOA rentals, reinvestment fees, water).

## Utah New Construction Notes

Last verified: 2026-11-08

Utah facts for a builder's purchase agreement: how it relates to the REPC, a side-by-side of REPC protections, deposits, construction liens, defect claims, arbitration, contractor licensing, property tax and districts. This is not legal advice. The signed contract, the recorded documents and the current statute control. Anything marked "confirm with" goes into the output as a question, never as a fact.

### The Builder's Form Is Not the REPC

- The REPC effective November 1, 2026 opens: "Utah law normally requires real estate licensees to use this form. If legal counsel for a buyer, seller, lessor, or lessee has prepared legal forms, a real estate licensee may instead choose to use those legal forms." The December 4, 2024 REPC opens: "Utah law requires real estate licensees to use this form. Buyer and Seller, however, may agree to alter or delete its provisions or to use a different form."
- Utah Code 61-2f-306(2)(b): a licensee "may fill out real estate forms prepared by legal counsel of the buyer, seller, lessor, or lessee." A builder's contract prepared by the builder's attorneys is that kind of form. Its section numbers and terms are its own.
- The Utah Association of REALTORS library lists a "New Construction REPC" and a "Change Order Addendum to New Construction REPC" (members only; their terms were not reviewed for this file). Whether a builder will use them is the builder's choice.
- Licensees don't draft contract language (see the Licensee Forms part of this file). Changes to a builder's form go between the builder's counsel and the buyer's real estate attorney.

### Deposits Held by the Builder

- Utah Administrative Code R162-2f-403a(17): a principal broker "may not release for construction purposes" deposit money held under an agreement to build a dwelling "unless the purchaser authorizes such disbursement in writing" (see the December 4, 2024 REPC part of this file, New Construction). That rule governs money a principal broker holds. It does not describe money a buyer pays straight to a builder.
- When a builder contract has the buyer pay deposits to the builder, and lets the builder use them, say so plainly and put it on the attorney list. Whether the money can be held by a title company instead is a question for the builder's rep.

### Construction Liens After a New Home Closes

- Utah Code 38-1a-502(1)(a): a person claiming a construction lien must record the notice of lien no later than 180 days after final completion of the original contract if no notice of completion is filed, or 90 days after a notice of completion is filed (but not later than 180 days after final completion). A subcontractor who works after the certificate of occupancy or final inspection has 180 days after finishing that work (38-1a-502(1)(b)).
- A notice of completion may be filed with the State Construction Registry by the owner, the original contractor, the construction lender, a surety, or the title company issuing a policy (38-1a-507(1)(a)).
- Utah's Residence Lien Restriction and Lien Recovery Fund Act (Title 38, Chapter 11) protects some owners of an "owner-occupied residence." Its definition of "owner" includes a person who "contracts with a real estate developer to buy a residence upon completion of the construction" or "purchases a residence from a real estate developer after completion" (38-11-102(16)). An owner-occupied residence is one occupied by the owner or the owner's tenant "as a primary or secondary residence within 180 days after the day on which the construction on the residence is complete" (38-11-102(17)).
- The protection depends on conditions, including a written contract with the real estate developer and payment in full under that contract (38-11-204(4)(a) and (b), applied through 38-11-107(1)).
- What to ask: whether a notice of completion has been or will be filed, whether the owner's title policy will cover construction liens recorded after closing, and how the lien statutes apply to this purchase. Confirm with the title company and a real estate attorney. Never tell the buyer they are protected.

### Construction Defect Claims and Time Limits

- Utah Code 78B-4-513(2): "Except as provided in Subsection (3), an action for defective design or construction is limited to breach of the contract, whether written or otherwise, including both express and implied warranties." Subsection (3) allows claims for damage to other property or physical personal injury caused by the defect. Only a person in privity of contract with the original contractor, architect, engineer or "the real estate developer" may bring the action, though contract rights can be assigned (78B-4-513(5) and (7)).
- For condominiums, an owner must first give the condominium developer written notice of the defect and a repair request, and may sue only if the developer doesn't complete the repairs within nine months (78B-4-513(8) and (9)).
- Utah Code 78B-2-225(3)(a): an action "based in contract or warranty shall be commenced within six years after the date of completion or abandonment of an improvement." Under 78B-2-225(3)(c), "If a contract or warranty expressly establishes a different period of limitations than this section, the action shall be commenced within that limitations period." Other actions generally have two years from discovery and may not be brought more than nine years after completion, with exceptions (78B-2-225(4) and (5)).
- "Completion" means substantial completion, set by the earliest of a certificate of substantial completion, a certificate of occupancy, or the date of first use or possession (78B-2-225(1)(c)).
- Why it matters for this review: because defect claims run mostly through the contract and its warranties, the warranty terms, any disclaimer of implied warranties, any contract deadline for bringing a claim, and the dispute resolution clause carry real weight. Quote each one and put it on the attorney list. Don't say how a court would read them.

### Arbitration

Utah Code 78B-11-107(1): "An agreement contained in a record to submit to arbitration any existing or subsequent controversy arising between the parties to the agreement is valid, enforceable, and irrevocable except upon a ground that exists at law or in equity for the revocation of a contract." The REPC itself has only optional or checkbox mediation (2026 Section 18; 2024 Section 15). Describe the builder's clause as written and send it to the attorney.

### The Builder's Contractor License

The Division of Professional Licensing (DOPL) links "Verify a License" to Utah's License Lookup Verification search: https://secure.utah.gov/llv/search/index.html. Suggest the agent look up the builder or its contractor entity by the exact name in the contract. Questions about what a license covers go to DOPL.

### Property Tax on a New Home

- Utah Code 59-2-103(2): property is "assessed and taxed at a uniform and equal rate on the basis of its fair market value, as valued on January 1, unless otherwise provided by law." A home finished partway through a year may have been valued on January 1 as a lot or a partly built home. Ask the title company how the closing proration is figured and ask the county assessor what the next bill is likely to be based on. Never estimate a tax.
- 59-2-103(3): residential property is allowed "a residential exemption equal to a 45% reduction in the value of the property," limited to one primary residence per household (59-2-103(6)).
- Property under construction can count as residential property if the county assessor determines it will be a primary residence (59-2-102(35)(b)(ii)), and the owner files a written declaration with the county assessor (59-2-103.5(4), version effective July 1, 2026). A county may require an application when ownership changes (59-2-103.5(1)). Confirm what the buyer must file, and by when, with the county assessor.

### Districts, HOA and the Lot

- Public infrastructure districts: use the Utah Property Notes part of this file (what a PID is, the levy limit, the recorded notice, and the seller disclosure of the expected annual cost at or before closing under Utah Code 57-1-49). Ask for that annual cost now, before the buyer signs.
- The November 2026 REPC's special assessment line (Section 8.2) covers assessments approved by the HOA, a municipality or a special improvement district before the Settlement Deadline. Per the Utah Property Notes part of this file, the PID reference was removed from that line, and a PID levy is better handled as a proration. A builder form may say nothing about either; say so.
- In a new community the builder, as declarant, may still control the HOA. The Community Association Act defines the "period of administrative control" as "the period during which the person who filed the association's governing documents or the person's successor in interest retains authority to: (a) appoint or remove members of the association's board of directors; or (b) exercise power or authority assigned to the association under the association's governing documents" (Utah Code 57-8a-102(22)). Ask when it ends and how the budget and reserves were set.
- A declaration recorded before the developer's first sale may restrict rentals without the statutory exemptions (see the Utah Property Notes part of this file).
- Water: ask the builder who provides culinary and secondary water and whether any water rights or shares come with the lot (see the Utah Property Notes part of this file).

### What Changed, and How It Reads

Compared with the December 4, 2024 form:

**Deadlines are in Section 4, and there are two financing deadlines.** The December 2024 form has one Financing & Appraisal Deadline (24(c)) and the Settlement Deadline at 24(d). The November 2026 form has a separate Appraisal Deadline (4(c)) and Financing Deadline (4(d)), and the Settlement Deadline is 4(e). A counter that says "the date in Section 4(b)" is changing the Due Diligence Deadline.

**Seller money toward the buyer's costs is a line in the contract.** Section 2.2: "Seller agrees to contribute at Settlement the amount of $___ to be applied at Buyer's discretion toward Buyer's Closing Costs as defined in Section 8.3(c), if applicable." Section 8.3(c) lets the buyer apply it to "(a) a permanent reduction, or temporary reduction, in the mortgage loan interest rate; (b) mortgage financing costs; (c) closing costs; and (d) Buyer's prepaid escrow funds," and says "Any unused portion of Seller's contribution may, at Buyer's option, be applied to any costs or expenses permitted by Buyer's lender (if applicable), including a reduction in the Purchase Price." A counter that changes seller money should be read against Section 2.2. The buyer's lender confirms how much the loan program allows.

**Home warranty** is Section 2.3: "[ ] WILL [ ] WILL NOT," with "Seller agrees to pay for a home warranty plan selected and ordered by Buyer not to exceed $___ at Settlement."

**Earnest money on a financing cancellation is a percentage.** Section 3(c)(i): "[ ] 50% of the Earnest Money Deposit; or [ ] ___ % of the Earnest Money Deposit described below shall be released to Seller if Buyer cancels the REPC in accordance with Section 12.3(a). If no box is checked, then the percentage shall be 50%." The December 2024 form uses a dollar blank in 8.3(b)(i). Show the dollar result of the percentage, with the math.

**Earnest money delivery.** Section 5: the buyer delivers the deposit to the Buyer's Brokerage "No later than four (4) calendar days after Acceptance," and the brokerage has four calendar days to deposit it in its trust account or with a title insurance company. "If the Earnest Money Deposit is not delivered or deposited within the timeframe described above, Seller may, in Seller's sole discretion, cancel the REPC." There is no Additional Earnest Money section in this form.

**Possession and holdover.** Possession is checked in 4(f): "Upon Recording," a number of hours after Recording, or a number of calendar days after Recording. Section 9.3 says a rental between the parties "shall be by separate written agreement or addendum to the REPC," and sets a holdover fee if the seller doesn't deliver possession: "$ ___ or $300 per day, whichever is greater," described as "liquidated damages."

**Brokerage compensation.** Section 2.1 sets any Seller's Compensation Contribution. Section 8.3(b): "If Seller's Compensation Contribution exceeds the written buyer-broker agreement, the excess amount shall be returned to Seller." Don't compute commissions without a net sheet.

**Acceptance requires receipt.** Section 23: Acceptance occurs only when "(a) Buyer or Seller has signed the offer or counteroffer where noted to indicate acceptance; and (b) the offer or counteroffer has been Received by the other party, or the other party's respective agent, or by the other party's branch broker or principal broker, no later than the applicable date referenced in Section 30 or as noted in the counteroffer." Section 22 says electronic delivery is "Received" when it "enters an information processing system (e.g., email server) that the recipient has designated or uses" for that purpose. The December 2024 form instead requires that the signing be "communicated."

**Weekends and holidays.** Section 25: "if any dates for the Earnest Money delivery, Earnest Money Deposit or the Settlement Deadline referenced in this REPC fall on a Saturday, Sunday, or legal holiday, performance shall be required on the next business day." That rule covers only those dates. The December 2024 form has no such rule.

**Deadlines and time of day.** Section 25 keeps "5:00 PM Mountain Time on the stated date" and calendar days "counted beginning on the day following the event which triggers the timing requirement (e.g., Acceptance)." Extensions "must be agreed to in writing by all parties."

**The offer's own deadline.** Section 30: "If Seller does not accept this offer by: ___ [ ] AM [ ] PM Mountain Time on ___ (Date), this offer shall lapse." A seller's counteroffer is checked on the COUNTEROFFER box under Section 30 and refers to "the attached ADDENDUM NO.___."

**Due diligence waiver.** Section 12.1(c): if the buyer doesn't cancel or resolve objections in writing by the Due Diligence Deadline in 4(b), the buyer "shall be deemed to have waived the Due Diligence Condition, and, except as provided in Sections 12.2(a) and 12.3(a), the Earnest Money Deposit shall become non-refundable." Moving 4(b) earlier moves that point earlier.

**Appraisal cancellation.** Section 12.2(a): the buyer may cancel with written notice "along with a complete copy of the appraisal, no later than the Appraisal Deadline referenced in Section 4(c)."

### What Stays the Same

- Changes still go on an approved addendum, and a licensee may not alter the REPC's printed or filled-in language to make a counteroffer (see the Licensee Forms part of this file).
- Prorations are made as of the Settlement Deadline (8.1), so a counter that moves Settlement moves the proration date.
- Closing still means Settlement plus loan funding and Recording, with funding and Recording "no later than four calendar days after Settlement" (9.2).

## Licensee Forms

Last verified: 2026-11-08. Sources: https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title61/Chapter2F/61-2f-S306.html and https://adminrules.utah.gov/Rule/325

### What a Licensee May Do

- **Fill out approved forms.** Utah Code 61-2f-306(1): "a real estate licensee may fill out only those legal forms approved by the commission and the attorney general, and those forms provided by statute." R162-2f-401a(17) points to the same statute.
- **Fill out forms drafted by a party's attorney.** 61-2f-306(2)(b): a licensee "may fill out real estate forms prepared by legal counsel of the buyer, seller, lessor, or lessee."
- **Fill out counsel-drafted forms when no state form exists.** 61-2f-306(2)(c): if the commission and attorney general "have not approved a specific form for the transaction," a licensee may fill out forms "prepared by any legal counsel, including legal counsel retained by the brokerage to develop these forms."
- **Closing documents.** 61-2f-306(2)(a): a principal broker may fill out any closing documents. A branch or associate broker may do so if the principal broker designates them.
- **Use an addendum for every change.** R162-2f-401a(18): a licensee shall "use an approved addendum form to make a counteroffer or any other modification to a contract." The Division's guidance is to write changes "into an approved addendum and included with the REPC."
- **Let the parties change the deal.** The REPC header says the parties "may agree to alter or delete its provisions or to use a different form." The licensee still makes those changes through an addendum.

### What a Licensee May Not Do

- **Alter the REPC itself.** R162-2f-401b(1)(p): a licensee may not "make a counteroffer by striking out, whiting out, substituting new language, or otherwise altering" the REPC's boilerplate or the language inserted to fill its blanks. The Division has warned about contracts "altered by hand, crossed out, whited out, or simply changes through written wording off to the side."
- **Draft contracts.** Division guidance on using the blank Addendum for repair requests: "unless you are an attorney, you are not authorized to draft and create contracts so use this option with care."
- **Patch a state form with a generic addendum.** For earnest money held by a title company, the Division said licensees "should not attempt to modify the REPC by striking through provisions or by filling in blank spaces on a generic addendum form." Use the specific state-approved addendum.
- **Hide terms.** R162-2f-401b(1)(d) bars preparing a document that does not reflect "the true terms of the transaction." R162-2f-401b(1)(f) bars a "double contract." The Division has said a repair agreement must become part of the purchase contract and go to the lender, title company and broker.
- **Skip a client's requested contingency.** The Division has said failing to include a client-requested contingency, or discouraging it, "is inconsistent with the duty of obedience."
- A 2026 Division disciplinary summary describes a licensee disciplined in part because the purchase contract "was not a state approved form and was not prepared by legal counsel."

### When an Attorney Should Draft or Review

- Any term that a state-approved form or the brokerage's counsel-approved forms do not already cover. Confirm with your broker before writing new language.
- **Seller financing.** The Seller Financing Addendum says brokers "are not qualified, nor licensed, to ensure that the terms of this Seller Financing Addendum comply with these laws" and that the parties "are strongly advised to carefully consult with a legal professional."
- Contracts for deed, lease options, assignments and other structures without a state form. Confirm with your broker which counsel-prepared forms the brokerage allows.
- Disputes over earnest money or whether a contract was formed. Send these to the broker; legal questions go to a real estate attorney.


## Utah Property Notes

Last verified: 2026-11-08. Not legal advice; the recorded documents and the current statute control. Sources are listed at the end of this part.

### Water Rights and Water Shares

#### The Terms

- A water right is real property in Utah. Water rights are bought and sold much like real estate.
- A water share is stock in a water company, such as an irrigation, canal, or ditch company. Shares are generally not transferred by deed. Each company has its own transfer steps. The most common: the seller endorses and delivers the stock certificate, and the buyer presents it to the company for a new certificate. Each company sets how much water a share delivers and what fees and assessments it charges.
- Culinary water is the indoor domestic supply. The state addendum notes call domestic use "indoor culinary" use.
- Secondary water is water that is not culinary and is delivered for irrigating landscaping or a garden. A secondary water supplier is an entity that supplies pressurized secondary water (Utah Code 73-10-34). Listings may call it pressurized irrigation or PI. Confirm the provider.
- Utah law requires secondary water suppliers to meter pressurized secondary water for each user by January 1, 2030, with some exemptions (Utah Code 73-10-34).
- Water rights not used for seven years are subject to forfeiture. A nonuse application can protect a right (Division of Water Rights FAQ, citing Utah Code 73-1-4).
- The county recorder is the office of record for title. The Division of Water Rights ownership record is not the official title record.

#### The Water Rights Addendum

- Utah Code 57-3-109 defines a water rights addendum as an addendum to a deed, in a form approved by the Legislature, that either identifies the water rights transferred or states that none are transferred.
- A person recording a deed may submit the addendum with it. The grantor (seller) completes and signs it. The grantee (buyer) signs to acknowledge receipt. The county recorder sends a copy to the state engineer.
- The Division of Water Rights publishes two versions: the Water Rights Addendum to Land Deeds and the Water Rights Addendum to Water Deeds.
- On the land deed addendum, the seller checks one box: all water rights used on the parcel are conveyed, only a portion is conveyed, no water rights are conveyed, or water rights are conveyed by a separate deed.
- Section A or B lists water right numbers. A partial conveyance states the portion in acre-feet and the uses (families, irrigated acres, livestock units).
- Section C holds the seller's disclosures: water company shares being endorsed and delivered (how many, which company), who provides culinary water, who provides outdoor water, that no water service is available, or other water facts.
- The form's notes say buying a water right does not guarantee the right is in good standing, that the seller has clear title, that the Division will recognize the ownership change, or that it will approve future changes. They encourage due diligence.
- If the Division does not update ownership from the recorded deed and addendum, the owner must file a Report of Water Right Conveyance (Utah Code 73-1-10(3)). The buyer must keep a current address on file with the Division.
- Not every appurtenant water right has a water right number.

#### What the REPC Says

- The purchase price includes the water rights and water shares, if any, that are the legal source of the seller's current water service. They transfer at closing by deed or other legal instrument.
- REPC effective November 1, 2026: Section 7.5 covers culinary or domestic, irrigation, secondary, and stock watering service. Seller Disclosures include evidence of the water rights or shares (Section 11(i)).
- REPC effective December 4, 2024: Section 1.4 covers culinary and irrigation service and has a line for excluded water rights or shares. Seller Disclosures include the same evidence (Section 7(i)).
- Both versions list water sources, availability, and quality as due diligence items.

#### What Buyers Should Ask

- Which water rights or shares come with the home: water right numbers, or the share count and company name.
- Who provides culinary water, and who provides outdoor or secondary water. Whether the secondary connection is metered and how it is billed.
- For shares: the company's transfer steps, transfer fees, annual assessments, and how much water a share delivers. Ask the company.
- For water rights: the status in the Division's records and any evidence of recent use.
- Whether the seller will sign the Water Rights Addendum at closing, and which box.
- Confirm with the title company, the water company, and the Division of Water Rights. For complex rights, the addendum notes point to attorneys, engineers, surveyors, and title professionals who work with water rights.

### HOA Rental Restrictions

Two statutes say almost the same thing. Utah Code 57-8a-209 covers community associations (lots). Utah Code 57-8-10.1 covers condominiums (units).

#### What an Association May Do

- Restrict the number and term of rentals, or prohibit rentals.
- The restriction must be in the recorded declaration (CC&Rs) or an amendment to it. One exception: a rule may set a minimum lease term of six months or less.
- Require the owner and renter to sign an association lease addendum.
- If at least 35% of units may be rentals, charge a rental owner up to $200 once every 12 months for added administrative costs. Notice, a board meeting, and a majority board vote come first.

#### What an Association May Not Require

Except in narrow cases, an association may not require a rental owner to:
- get approval of a renter,
- hand over a rental application, credit report, background check, or proof of the renter's age,
- pay an extra assessment, fine, or fee because the unit is a rental (other than the $200 fee above),
- use the association's lease, or get the association's approval of a lease.

The narrow cases are a court order or discovery, and an association whose declaration lawfully restricts occupancy by a certain class of individuals, when it uses the information to check compliance.

For community associations only: an association may not restrict or prohibit renting an internal accessory dwelling unit that meets land use, building, health, and fire codes (57-8a-209(10)). The condominium section has no matching provision.

#### Required Exemptions

An association that restricts rentals or charges the rental fee must exempt:
- an owner in the military, during deployment,
- a unit occupied by the owner's parent, child, or sibling,
- an owner relocated by an employer for two years or less,
- a unit owned by an entity and occupied by a person with voting rights and at least 25% ownership,
- a unit owned by an estate planning trust for the current resident or the resident's parent, child, or sibling.

#### Grandfathering

- An owner who was renting before the restriction took effect may keep renting, without the rental fee. For condominiums, the cutoff is when the restriction is recorded with the county recorder.
- That right ends when the owner moves in, when an owner or officer of an owning entity or trust moves in, or when the unit is transferred.
- A transfer is a deed conveyance, a grant of a life estate, or, for an entity owner, a transfer of more than 75% of the ownership interests in 12 months.
- A buyer does not inherit the seller's grandfathered rental status, because the sale is a transfer.

#### Exceptions That Change the Answer

- A declaration recorded before the developer sold the first lot or unit may restrict rentals without the exemptions and grandfathering above. This matters in new communities.
- Unanimous approval by all owners allows a restriction without the exemptions.
- Timeshares are excluded.
- Older associations: parts of these sections do not apply to an association formed before May 12, 2009 (for condos, an initial declaration recorded before that date), unless it adopted or amended a rental restriction on or after May 12, 2015. Confirm with the HOA manager or a real estate attorney.
- These sections do not limit age requirements for housing for older persons.

#### What to Ask For

- The recorded CC&Rs and every amendment with a rental restriction, with recording dates. Bylaws, rules, and fee and fine schedules. The November 2026 REPC lists the CC&Rs, bylaws, fee and fine schedules, and rules as Seller Disclosures (Section 11(d)). The December 2024 REPC lists the CC&Rs and rules (Section 7(d)).
- HOA minutes, budget, and financial statement. The November 2026 REPC adds the reserve analysis (Section 11(e)). The December 2024 REPC lists minutes, budget, and financial statement (Section 7(e)).
- The current rental count against any cap, any waitlist, any minimum lease term, any rental fee, and any required lease addendum. Ask the HOA manager.
- Whether the seller is renting under grandfathering, and a reminder that it ends at the sale.
- How the rules apply to a specific plan: confirm with a real estate attorney.

### HOA Reinvestment Fees (Utah Code 57-1-46)

A reinvestment fee is a fee a common interest association charges a buyer or seller because the property transfers. It is dedicated to benefiting the common areas or the burdened property.

#### The Cap After H.B. 306 (2026)

- 2026 H.B. 306, Reinvestment Fee Amendments, took effect May 6, 2026.
- For a reinvestment fee covenant recorded on or after May 6, 2026, the fee may not exceed 0.5% of the property's value. In a low-amenity association it may not exceed 0.25%.
- A low-amenity association has only detached single-family homes and does not provide capital-intensive infrastructure maintenance to members.
- Property in a large master planned development is not subject to the cap. That is an approved development of at least 500 acres or 500 units with a commitment to fund or maintain items such as common infrastructure, amenities, or open space.
- Before H.B. 306, this subsection capped fees at 0.5% for covenants recorded on or after March 16, 2010, with the same master planned development exception. H.B. 306 changed the recording date in the cap to May 6, 2026. How the cap applies today to a covenant recorded between 2010 and May 6, 2026: confirm with the title company or a real estate attorney.

#### Other Rules in the Statute

- Since May 6, 2026, an association may not impose a reinvestment fee unless the declaration or a reinvestment fee covenant authorizes it, a majority of voting interests approves it (or a higher share if the documents require), and, outside a large master planned development, at least 50% of the fee goes into reserve funds.
- The association sets the amount under its documents, after notice. Members can remove or change the fee at a special meeting where at least 51% of voting interests attend and vote.
- A reinvestment fee covenant recorded on or after March 16, 2010 is void unless a separate notice of the covenant is recorded in the county.
- For a covenant recorded on or after March 16, 2010, the fee cannot be enforced on an involuntary transfer, a transfer by court order, a bona fide transfer to a family member within three degrees of blood relation (with proof), a transfer at death, or a transfer by a financial institution (except the association's direct transfer costs, up to $250).
- A transfer fee covenant recorded on or after March 16, 2010 is void.
- An administrative setup fee imposed on or after May 7, 2025 is void unless the association uses it only for costs of the transfer.
- H.B. 306 requires an association registering with the Department of Commerce to state whether it charges a reinvestment or transfer fee. The HOA Ombudsman's website must say that no one may rely on that registry disclosure when preparing purchase documents. Get the fee from the HOA and the title company.

#### In the REPC

- The parties choose who pays "change of ownership fees" (transfer fees, community enhancement fees, HOA reinvestment fees) by checkbox: Section 8.3(a) in the November 2026 REPC, Section 4.3(c) in the December 2024 REPC.
- The November 2026 REPC adds that HOA buyer setup or administrative fees are the buyer's and are not change of ownership fees.
- If a quoted fee looks above the cap that applies, flag it for the title company to confirm. Do not call it unlawful.

### Public Infrastructure Districts (Utah Code Title 17D, Chapter 4)

#### What They Are

- A public infrastructure district (PID) is a special district created under the Public Infrastructure District Act. It is a political subdivision of the state, separate from the city or county.
- A creating entity (a county, municipality, basic special district, or development authority) approves the district's governing document.
- Creation requires a petition signed by 100% of the surface property owners in the area. A development authority may create one with the written consent of all surface owners.
- A board runs it. The governing document may move board seats from appointment to election based on milestones it sets.
- The governing document sets the boundaries, the property tax levy rate limit, the debt limit, and the infrastructure the district will build. Raising the levy rate limit needs the consent of 100% of surface property owners.
- Since May 6, 2026, the board must dissolve the district after its debts are paid, its obligations are met, and its improvements are completed, accepted, and transferred. A district created by a development authority that provides ongoing services is excepted (17D-4-401).

#### How They Are Paid

- A PID issues bonds. A PID bond must mature within 40 years of issuance.
- The bonds are repaid by a property tax levy on property inside the district. For all purposes, including limited tax bonds, the levy may not exceed 0.015 per dollar of taxable value. General obligation bonds and a few listed cases are outside that limit (17D-4-303).
- A PID may also charge fees set by a fee schedule (17D-4-302).
- On nonpayment of a PID tax, fee, or charge, the district may add a property tax penalty at an annual rate of 0.07, on top of other lawful penalties (17D-4-304).

#### Disclosure Rules for Buyers

- Recorded notice: within 30 days after the Lieutenant Governor certifies the district, the board must record a notice with the county recorder. It gives the boundaries, says the governing document is on file with the creating entity, says the district may levy a property tax to finance and repay infrastructure, and states the maximum rate. The district may not levy a tax or collect a fee until that notice is recorded (17D-4-303(3) and (4)).
- Seller disclosure: since May 6, 2026, in a sale of residential property inside a PID, the seller or the seller's representative must make sure the expected annual cost of the district's final tax rate, from the last equalized assessment rolls, is in a disclosure document at or before closing (Utah Code 57-1-49, enacted by 2026 H.B. 507).
- In the REPC: per the Real Estate Commission's September 23, 2026 minutes, the PID reference was removed from the special assessment line (Section 8.2) of the November 2026 REPC, so a PID levy is not treated as a special assessment there. UAR's counsel said the levy is better handled as a proration under Section 8.1.
- "At or before closing" is late for a buyer's decision. Ask during due diligence.

#### What to Ask

- Is the property inside a PID? Check the title commitment for a recorded notice.
- The governing document (from the creating entity), the maximum levy rate, and the current annual amount on the tax bill (from the county).
- Confirm with the title company and the county treasurer or assessor.

### New Construction: Utah Rules Confirmed

- PIDs: the recorded notice and the 57-1-49 seller disclosure above apply to new homes inside a district.
- Rental restrictions: a declaration recorded before the developer's first sale may restrict rentals without the statutory exemptions (57-8a-209(5), 57-8-10.1(5)).
- Contract form: the November 2026 REPC says Utah law normally requires licensees to use it, but a licensee may use forms prepared by a party's legal counsel instead. A builder's purchase agreement is not the REPC. Read its own terms, and send contract questions to a real estate attorney.
- Land sales: Utah's Uniform Land Sales Practices Act (57-11) does not apply to a unit with a residential building on it, or one the seller is legally obligated to finish building within two years (57-11-4(1)(b)). For a vacant lot with no build obligation, confirm with the Division of Real Estate's land sales program.
- Radon: EPA notes that radon-reducing features can be built into a new home. Ask the builder what is included. Whether the city or county requires them: confirm with the local building department.
- Water: ask the builder who provides culinary and secondary water, and whether any water rights or shares transfer.
- Warranties, liens, and other builder disclosures: no other Utah-specific new-construction disclosure statute was confirmed for this file. Confirm with a real estate attorney and the title company.
