# New Construction Guide

Two parts: the Review Guide (how to read a builder's purchase agreement, term by term) and the Drafting Guide (questions for the builder's rep, the attorney list, key dates, the buyer email and Check before sending).

## Review Guide: Reading a Builder's Purchase Agreement

Last verified: 2026-11-08 (the federal ILSA note under Completion and Delay; the rest of this file is reading method, not law)

How to read a builder's contract, what to capture for each key term, and what to watch for. Facts about RESPA and incentives are in `lender-incentives-respa.md` (in this project); Utah law and the REPC comparison are in `utah-new-construction-and-repc.md` (in this project). Describe what the documents say. Never call a term standard, typical, illegal or unenforceable: that is an attorney's call.

### Reading Order

1. The first page and the signature page: parties (the selling entity's exact legal name), lot and plan, price lines, the contract or effective date, any lot hold or signing deadline.
2. Definitions and the conflict or order-of-precedence clause: which document controls when the body, an exhibit and an addendum disagree.
3. Every addendum and exhibit. Builders often put money terms (deposits, incentives, option payments) there.
4. The body, section by section, including the back pages: default, dispute resolution, warranty, limits on claims, assignment, recording, entire agreement.
5. Plat notes, CC&Rs, district disclosures and the title commitment, if uploaded.

Mark every dollar amount, every date or trigger ("within 10 days after Notice of Completion"), and every phrase that shifts risk: "non-refundable," "sole remedy," "in lieu of," "waives," "at Seller's sole discretion," "estimated," "subject to change," "time is of the essence," "no oral representations."

### Build Stage

- **To be built (dirt sale):** the longest timeline. Pre-drywall and final inspections are both still possible. Watch for price escalation clauses, open-ended completion dates and when option selections lock.
- **Under construction:** ask what stage the home is at; a pre-drywall inspection may already be impossible.
- **Completed (spec, quick move-in, inventory):** closing can be days away. A full inspection before closing is the main protection. Incentives on finished homes are often tied to closing by a date; read that date closely.

### Key Terms Checklist

For each term: what to capture, then what to watch for. Quote the contract where the exact words matter and give the page and section.

**1. Price.** Base price, lot premium, options and upgrades, total. Any clause that can change the price (cost escalation, a price "subject to change" until a milestone, a change in plan or elevation). If a clause caps the increase as a percent or a dollar amount, show the largest increase it allows, with the math. Watch for: a total that doesn't add up, a price that can rise after signing, an appraisal clause that makes the buyer pay any gap.

**2. Deposits.** Each payment, its due date or trigger, who holds it (title escrow, a brokerage trust account, or the builder), whether the builder may use it for construction or any purpose, when it becomes nonrefundable, and the events that return it. Watch for: deposits paid to the builder directly, "non-refundable upon acceptance," option payments that are never refundable even when the earnest money is, and a short cancellation window with a fee.

**3. Completion and delay.** Estimated completion date or range; whether it is guaranteed; extensions (weather, labor, materials, governmental delays, force majeure) and whether they have a limit; any outside date; the buyer's remedies for delay (cancellation, refund, a per day payment, none); the builder's right to cancel; the notice of completion and how many days the buyer has to close after it. Watch for: no outside date, "Seller shall have no liability for delays," and a short window to close after notice, because the buyer's lender and rate lock must fit it (a lender question).

*Federal note (any state), the Interstate Land Sales Full Disclosure Act (ILSA):* 15 U.S.C. 1702(a)(2) says the Act does not apply to "the sale or lease of any improved land on which there is a residential, commercial, condominium, or industrial building, or the sale or lease of land under a contract obligating the seller or lessor to erect such a building thereon within a period of two years." Builder contracts sometimes promise completion within two years for this reason. When a contract for a home not yet built has no firm obligation to finish within two years (for example, unlimited extensions), list it for the attorney as a question; don't say what follows.

**4. Change orders and upgrades.** Selection deadlines and cut-off dates, change fees, when payment is due, refundability, the builder's right to substitute materials or change plans, and disclaimers about models, renderings and brochures. Watch for: 100% nonrefundable payment for options on order, and "models may include features not included in the price."

**5. Inspection rights.** Whether the buyer may hire a third-party inspector; which inspections (pre-drywall or pre-insulation, final, after closing before the warranty ends); scheduling and notice rules; insurance or license requirements for the inspector; whether the buyer may attend; what the builder must do about findings; whether an inspection gives any right to cancel. Watch for: no third-party inspections during construction, inspections only after the notice of completion, findings fixed only "in Seller's discretion" or only against the builder's own standards.

**6. Walkthrough.** Orientation or walkthrough timing, the punch list, how long the builder has to finish items after closing, whether open items can delay closing, and escrow holdbacks for unfinished work (whether one is allowed is the lender's call). Watch for: "punch list items are not a condition of closing."

**7. Warranty, as written.** Who provides it (the builder or a third-party company), each coverage period (workmanship and materials, systems, structural), exclusions, how to make a claim, whether it replaces implied warranties ("in lieu of all other warranties, express or implied"), any time limit to bring a claim, and whether it transfers to a later owner. Quote any disclaimer and any claim deadline. If a warranty booklet is referred to but not uploaded, list it as missing; don't fill in its terms.

**8. Dispute resolution.** Mediation, arbitration (binding or not), who picks the arbitrator, the rules and location, who pays fees, jury and class action waivers, a small claims carve-out, whether warranty claims are covered, and any deadline to start a claim. Describe it and send it to the attorney. Don't say whether it would be enforced.

**9. Financing and appraisal.** Loan application and approval deadlines, whether the builder must approve the lender, what happens to each deposit if the loan is denied, whether the purchase depends on the appraisal, and who pays an appraisal gap. Rate locks, loan terms and loan program limits are lender questions.

**10. Incentives.** Amount, what it can pay for, every condition, what happens if a condition isn't met, and the dollar difference between the two outcomes. See `lender-incentives-respa.md` (in this project).

**11. HOA and CC&Rs.** Dues and what they cover, setup, capitalization or working capital fees, reinvestment or transfer fees, the builder's (declarant's) control of the board and when it ends, rental and use restrictions, architectural approval, fencing and landscaping deadlines. Only what the documents say.

**12. Special districts and assessments.** The district's name and type, the maximum levy, the current levy or annual amount, fees, bonds and their term, and where each appears (plat note, recorded notice, title commitment, disclosure). Never estimate a tax.

**13. Buyer agent registration.** When and how the buyer's agent must register the buyer (often at the first visit), forms, deadlines, the compensation terms as written, and what voids them. Check against the buyer agreement rules in the buyer agreement rules (the brokerage may not receive more than the buyer's written agreement allows; confirm with the broker). Don't compute compensation.

**14. Everything else worth a line when present:** default remedies on both sides (compare them), the builder's right to cancel, assignment, recording bans, non-disparagement or review limits, risk of loss before closing, title and survey, who pays which closing costs, the entire agreement clause ("no oral representations"), and any promise the agent mentions that the documents don't contain.

### General Mode: Standard Protections to Compare Against

Without a state form to compare against, check whether the builder's contract gives the buyer each of these, and tell the agent to check the state's standard resale contract with their broker:
- An inspection or due diligence period with a right to cancel and get the earnest money back
- Financing and appraisal conditions with a refund of the deposit
- Seller disclosures and a title commitment to review by a deadline
- Earnest money held by a neutral party (title escrow or a brokerage trust account)
- Default remedies that are as strong for the buyer as for the seller
- A final walk-through before closing
- Mediation before a lawsuit, and access to court
- Risk of loss staying with the seller until closing
- Written deadlines with a clear time of day

### Ranking the Top Three Risks

Rank by:
1. Money that can't come back (nonrefundable deposits and option payments, with the running total)
2. Rights given up at signing that can't be fixed later (arbitration and waivers, warranty disclaimers, short claim deadlines, no inspection right)
3. Dates that land soon (the lot hold or signing deadline, loan application deadlines, a closing date tied to an incentive)
4. Conditions that can cost the incentive, in dollars

Name each risk with its dollar amount or date and its page.

## Drafting Guide: Questions, Attorney Items, Key Dates and the Buyer Email

How to write the parts of the output that other people read. This file is method only; facts live in the other references.

### Questions for the Builder's Rep

- One question per line, tied to a page and section: "Section 7.2 (page 3) says deposits are paid to Seller. Can the earnest money be held by the title company instead?"
- Ask for every document the contract refers to that isn't in the stack: the warranty booklet, the Affiliated Business Arrangement Disclosure, the CC&Rs, the HOA budget, the district's annual cost, the option price sheet.
- Ask for every promise the agent mentions that the documents don't contain, and ask that it go in the contract or an addendum: "You mentioned the builder will fix anything our inspector finds. Section 18 says no oral promises are binding. Can that go in writing?"
- Ask about dates the contract leaves open: the estimated completion, the outside date, when the notice of completion comes, how many days the buyer has to close after it.
- Neutral wording. Never accuse the builder, and never ask the rep a legal question ("is this enforceable") or a loan question ("is your rate better").
- Builder reps work for the builder. The questions are for information; anything the buyer wants changed goes through the attorney.

### For a Real Estate Attorney

A short list the agent can hand to the buyer's attorney. For each item: the page and section, a quote or a close paraphrase of what it says, and a plain question. Examples of what belongs here:
- Deposits paid to the builder and usable for construction; nonrefundable steps
- Binding arbitration, jury and class action waivers, fee splits, a contract deadline for bringing claims
- A warranty that replaces implied warranties, and its exclusions
- No completion deadline, unlimited extensions, or no remedy for delay (and, for a home not yet built, the federal ILSA two-year question)
- Price escalation or "subject to change" price terms
- Default remedies that differ for buyer and builder
- A requirement to use a particular lender, title or escrow company, or conflicting statements about it (RESPA questions)
- Anything about liens, title or assessments that the title company can't answer

Never write proposed clause language, and never say whether a clause is enforceable or legal. Introduce the list with: "Not legal advice. These are the clauses a real estate attorney should review with the buyer before signing."

### Key Dates

- Label the table: "For strategy; confirm dates with your transactions team."
- Every date comes from the documents or the agent's message. Where a date is counted from an event ("within 5 days after the Contract Date"), show it as counted from that event, or compute it from a date the agent gave and say so. If the event hasn't happened (the contract isn't signed yet), write it as "Contract Date + 5 days."
- Count calendar days from today to each fixed date. Give the weekday. Check weekdays in code.
- A signing or lot hold deadline within 48 hours goes first in the output, in bold.

### The Buyer Email

Write as the agent, in first person, to the buyer. Plain, warm, specific. It explains; it doesn't decide.

- **Subject** in Title Case, such as "What The Builder Contract Says Before You Sign" or "Builder Contract Review For Lot 47"
- **A bolded bottom line:** the two or three things that make this contract different, and what the buyer needs to decide by when
- **What You'd Be Putting At Risk:** each payment and when it becomes nonrefundable, with the running total
- **How This Differs From A Resale Contract:** three to five points in plain English (for example, no right to cancel after an inspection, no appraisal protection, binding arbitration instead of court)
- **The Incentive:** the amount and each condition, and what changes if the buyer uses another lender, in dollars from the documents. No advice on the loan. Say that their lenders can lay out the full cost of each option side by side, and that the choice is theirs.
- **Next Steps:** numbered, each with a date from the documents; always include "have a real estate attorney review the contract before you sign" and the questions going to the builder's rep
- Under 350 words, short paragraphs, no em dashes, none of "stunning," "dream home" or "don't hesitate to reach out"
- Don't tell the buyer whether to sign, whether a term is fair, or which lender to use
- No account numbers, loan numbers, or wire instructions; if a document has wire instructions, the email may say only that the buyer should confirm any wiring by phone directly with the title company at a number they look up themselves
- Sign with the agent's name if known; otherwise [Agent Name]

### Check Before Sending

List every date, dollar amount, percentage, page reference and quoted term that appears in the money table, key terms, incentive section, questions, attorney list, key dates and email. For each, say where to verify it ("Purchase Agreement page 2, Section 4.1"). Mark computed numbers as computed and show the math ("$10,000 + $10,337.50 = $20,337.50"). Mark any placeholder still in the drafts.
