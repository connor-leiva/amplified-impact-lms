The Forum AI Owner Implementation Cohort
Recommendation in brief
Design this as eight weeks of installing and using finished, real-estate-specific workflows, not
an eight-week tour of AI features or a blank-page skill-writing course. The paid outcome should
be a team owner and an operating partner who can use their own business information to
make decisions, turn those decisions into work, and maintain the workflows without you.
My judgment: $5,000 is defensible for this delivery model, but not for eight template
walkthroughs plus optional Q&A. It is not yet validated pricing: we do not have evidence of
these members’ willingness to pay, implementation results, or realized financial returns. The
curriculum below is designed to earn the price, not rationalize it.
Your concern about free training is justified. OpenAI now offers free, self-paced courses
covering practical AI use, building with AI, and leading adoption, so access to general AI
instruction is not a strong differentiator (OpenAI Academy course documentation).
The offer should therefore sell four things together:
    Implementation: Workflows configured around the member’s actual team, data,
    responsibilities, and standards.
    Judgment: Knowing which questions to ask, which outputs to distrust, and what action the
    information warrants.
    Adoption: Scheduled use, an accountable operator, and feedback on evidence from the
    business.
    Independence: The ability to adapt, test, repair, and hand off a supplied workflow, then
    extend the approach to a new business need.
Keep the confirmed delivery structure: one owner plus one operator; eight 90-minute live
builds; eight separate 60-minute office hours; all group delivery, no private coaching calls;
January start and March completion. Treat $5,000 in the room and $7,500 listed as working
prices pending Spring's approval, not settled terms. Cohort one is a Forum-room invitation;
whether a VIP guest must join the Forum to enroll remains unresolved.

The promise and the evidence behind it

A credible completion promise
Use this as the internal design target: “By the end of eight weeks, your team will have a
repeatable AI-assisted operating rhythm, a checked view of production and lead-source
economics, and a named operator who can maintain and extend the workflows.”
Do not promise a completely automated business, fully accurate profit by source regardless of
the underlying books, a fixed number of additional closings, or implementation across every
CRM integration. Those promises would exceed the scope and the evidence.
Graduation should require:
    Four core routines in use: Daily prioritization, weekly numbers review, weekly decision-to-
    task follow-through, and pipeline exception review. Each must show at least three real
    operating cycles, with daily cycles acceptable for the daily routine.
    One checked economics review: A completed baseline, a documented data-quality
    assessment, and at least one source decision or a justified decision to collect more data.
    One coaching and one delegation example: Actual use with a team member, not just a
    generated document.
    One independently adapted skill: A supplied workflow materially configured to the team's
    business that passes a normal case, a missing-information case, and a failure case. Blank-
    page authoring is an advanced option, not a graduation requirement.
    One handoff and continuation plan: A maintainer other than the instructor, refresh
    instructions, fallback procedures, and a 90-day adoption plan.
These are operating standards, not guaranteed financial outcomes. A participant can attend all
sessions and still be “not yet implemented”; attendance and implementation should be
reported separately.

Why this learning design
Workplace-training research supports taking the work environment seriously: a meta-analysis
found positive associations between peer, supervisor, and organizational support and training
transfer, while explicitly cautioning that the evidence was correlational (Hughes and colleagues,
published online 2019, journal issue 2020). My application is to pair the owner and operator, reserve
calendar time for use, and make peer troubleshooting part of the program.
A 2026 randomized study of 52 software developers found lower immediate comprehension
scores with AI assistance, while patterns involving conceptual questions and explanation were
associated with stronger learning; the authors caution that the study was small, short-term,
and specific to coding (Anthropic research, January 29, 2026). This does not establish what will
happen with real estate owners, but it argues against treating a successful AI-generated build as
proof that its user understands it.
Retrieval-practice research in schools and classrooms also supports asking learners to retrieve
and apply knowledge rather than only watch demonstrations, although it does not establish a
business-cohort effect size (2021 systematic review indexed by ERIC). My application is a short
explain-back every week and an independent adaptation of a supplied workflow in week 7.

The dependencies matter. Week 1 follows through on the Saturday finance promise and
establishes the baseline; week 2 creates daily use; week 3 converts numbers and commitments
into decisions; weeks 4–6 improve execution, coaching, and delegation; week 7 tests
independence; week 8 documents results and continuation.
Release one flagship workflow per week. Supporting skills are preassembled components or
optional extensions, not separate homework projects; the owner should experience one useful
business outcome, not an inventory of two or three new tools.
I would not lead with recruiting, content creation, or a quarterly packet. Those can be useful,
but they do not address the owner’s central operating blind spots early enough. I would also not
start with a complete accounting cleanup: it can consume the entire cohort before members
experience a useful workflow.

The weekly curriculum
For every week, distinguish “working by the end of the live session” from “implemented by the
end of the week.” Members receive the finished base skill in advance; live building means
configuring, testing, and installing it into their business. Actual use, review, and a repeatable
trigger establish implementation.
Week 1: Know the numbers without pretending the data is cleaner than it is
    Build: A one-page weekly production scorecard plus a scoped economics review covering
    the team total and its three largest identifiable lead sources. Use a prepared calculation
    template, not arithmetic invented in chat.
    Skills: Lead-Source Economics Review is the flagship; Weekly Production Report supplies
    the prepared scorecard. Capture the most important exceptions for the Morning Brief
    introduced next week.
    Why it matters: An owner needs to distinguish production, revenue, acquisition cost,
    contribution, and data uncertainty before changing spend or pressuring agents.
    How to use it: The operator refreshes the weekly production view; the owner reviews
    exceptions at a fixed weekly time. Refresh source economics after the books are sufficiently
    closed, usually as a separate monthly routine, rather than pretending the P&L is live.
    Live exercise: Two sources have different lead costs, close rates, and team-retained
    commissions. Members decide what they can conclude, what they cannot, and what
    further evidence is needed.
    Working at the end of class: Production totals reconciled to the designated source, a first
    economics calculation for at least one usable source, explicit unknowns, and one decision
    question.
    Working by week’s end: A team baseline, the best available three-source view, and either a
    justified action or a dated data-repair assignment. Freeze the baseline definition for one
    financial metric, preferably CPL, and time the actual reporting task including preparation
    and review.
    Acceptance test: Every key number has a source and period; denominators are defined; no
    missing costs are silently treated as zero; the owner can distinguish contribution from net
    profit.
    Between-session assignment: Review disputed totals and allocations with the person
    responsible for the books. Choose one restrained experiment or tracking improvement, not
    a wholesale marketing-budget change.
Limit the live build to one preconfigured view and one worked source. Members with
incomplete inputs finish on the supplied practice dataset and enter a visible “data repair
required” status; they have not yet completed business implementation.

Week 2: Own the day without creating another inbox
    Build: A one-page Morning Brief identifying no more than three owner priorities,
    exceptions requiring a decision, and the next owned actions. Use the week 1 scorecard plus
    a current task register; add calendar information only if already available.
    Skills: Morning Brief is the flagship; a narrow Task Sync is its supporting component. Start
    Task Sync with one authorized meeting transcript or email thread, not a fully connected
    inbox.
    Why it matters: The goal is not a prettier summary. It is fewer unowned commitments and
    a consistent way for the owner to decide what deserves attention.
    How to use it: The operator refreshes the inputs at a fixed time. The owner reviews the
    brief for ten minutes and accepts only tasks with a named owner, due date, and definition
    of done.
    Live exercise: Include a transcript saying “someone should follow up next week.” The skill
    must flag missing ownership and timing rather than invent them; the member resolves the
    ambiguity.
    Working at the end of class: A saved skill, one checked brief, and a single task destination.
    Use Granola notes and ClickUp if already adopted and authorized; manual paste is a valid
    fallback, not an excuse to introduce another task system.
    Working by week’s end: Five brief uses, one reconciled meeting-to-task run, and no
    duplicate task board. Record preparation and review time separately.
    Acceptance test: Every proposed action traces to an input; ambiguities remain visible; the
    owner can explain why the top three items were selected.
    Between-session assignment: Run it on actual work, mark what was ignored or wrong, and
    remove low-value sections. Add connections only after the output proves useful.
The owner must personally review and approve at least one real run during class, even when
the operator handles setup. Delegating maintenance is desirable; delegating all understanding
defeats the owner-learning requirement.

Week 3: Make the weekly meeting produce a decision
    Build: A meeting packet from week 1's scorecard, week 2's commitments, and an issue
    queue. Each issue includes the observed gap, supporting evidence, business impact,
    decision needed, and responsible person.
    Skills: L10 Meeting Prep, IDS Issue Writer, and Task Sync. Offer “weekly leadership
    meeting” labels for teams that do not use EOS.
    Why it matters: Reports have little value if they do not change a decision or a behavior. The
    skill should shorten preparation and improve the issues discussed, not add another
    meeting.
    How to use it: The operator prepares the packet the day before the existing leadership
    meeting. The owner chooses the highest-priority issue; AI can help frame alternatives but
    does not make the leadership decision.
    Live exercise: Convert “agents need to work harder” into a specific issue tied to a measured
    gap. Separate observed facts from possible causes.
    Working at the end of class: A usable agenda, one evidence-backed issue, and a decision-
    log template.
    Working by week’s end: One actual meeting with a recorded decision, an owner, a due date,
    and a follow-up check. Preserve “no action” decisions and the reasons for them.
    Acceptance test: The issue cites evidence, the decision is explicit, and the action appears in
    the same task system used in week 2.
    Between-session assignment: Compare preparation time with baseline and verify follow-
    through at the next meeting. Count closed decisions and overdue actions, not the number
    of generated pages.
Use a deliberately flawed issue statement during class. Members should challenge the diagnosis
before asking AI to polish it.

Week 4: Find pipeline exceptions and act on them
    Build: A Deal Status Digest that highlights overdue next actions, missing owners, stale
    stages, unconfirmed dates, and discrepancies between relevant records. Start with active
    deals and a small prioritized opportunity segment.
    Skills: Deal Status Digest; CRM Relationship Linker only to reconcile identifiers or propose
    reviewable relationships needed for this workflow. Task Sync carries approved actions
    forward.
    Why it matters: The output should direct attention to execution gaps, not restate every
    record in the CRM. It should distinguish a missing update from an actual stalled deal.
    How to use it: The operator runs the digest before an existing pipeline review. Owners or
    responsible agents confirm the exceptions and record a next action in the CRM or
    established task destination.
    Live exercise: Include two people with similar names and a deal with conflicting dates. The
    workflow must stop and request confirmation rather than merge people or assert an
    unverified deadline.
    Working at the end of class: A ranked exception list with record references and proposed
    next steps. No automatic merges, record edits, messages, or contract interpretation.
    Working by week’s end: Review the top five exceptions, or all if fewer than five; document
    each disposition and owner. Re-run the digest to see which exceptions remain.
    Acceptance test: Dates and stages trace to records, suspected matches are marked as
    uncertain, and missing source information is not presented as a real-world event.
    Between-session assignment: Track verified exceptions resolved, not “deals saved.” Use the
    human-maintained transaction system as the authority for contractual dates and
    obligations.
Keep relationship enrichment or full-database cleanup outside the core build. It is too easy for
this week to become a CRM repair project.

Week 5: Turn the scorecard into a coaching conversation
    Build: An Agent 1:1 Coaching Prep packet for one agent using agreed activity definitions,
    production context, prior commitments, and the agent’s own perspective.
    Skills: Agent 1:1 Coaching Prep and Weekly Production Report. Agent-facing Appointment
    Prep, Listing Appointment Follow-Up, or a comparable skill may be selected for a later
    pilot, but is not a second full-class build.
    Why it matters: Owners need to diagnose the difference between missing activity, weak
    conversion, poor process, and incomplete tracking. AI should help prepare a better
    conversation, not issue automated judgments about people.
    How to use it: Run before the existing 1:1. Verify the numbers, ask the agent about context,
    agree on one behavior to change, and set the next review date.
    Live exercise: Compare two agents with similar closings but different activities and
    opportunity mix. Members explain why the same coaching prescription may be wrong.
    Working at the end of class: One checked packet, three diagnostic questions, and one
    proposed behavior experiment.
    Working by week’s end: Two real coaching conversations, each ending with an agent-
    owned commitment and a follow-up date. A small team can use two conversations with the
    same agent.
    Acceptance test: The packet separates facts, hypotheses, and questions; it does not infer
    motivation, character, or protected characteristics from activity data.
    Between-session assignment: Invite two willing agents, or one on a very small team, into a
    limited workflow pilot. Pick one upcoming task, record a baseline, and agree on a quality
    standard.
This is the first bridge to agent training, but not a pitch. The owner is learning how to sponsor
adoption; the agent pilot will test whether the chosen behavior is actually useful.

Week 6: Delegate the deliverable, not just the task
    Build: Define one recurring responsibility currently stuck with the owner, then configure
    Task Drafts to produce its first draft from approved inputs. Good examples include a weekly
    internal update, meeting follow-up, or recruiting interview summary.
    Skills: Seat Definition Writer, Task Drafts, and Task Sync.
    Why it matters: A task labeled “handle this” does not define quality or decision authority.
    AI can draft work, but a human still needs to own the result and know when to escalate.
    How to use it: The owner defines the outcome, boundaries, and acceptance standard. The
    operator or delegated team member runs the skill, checks the draft, and submits it through
    the existing approval process.
    Live exercise: Compare “prepare our team update” with a specification naming audience,
    required evidence, prohibited information, deadline, and approval authority.
    Working at the end of class: A responsibility card, a tested first draft, and an explicit review
    checklist.
    Working by week’s end: The new owner completes one actual cycle and the business owner
    accepts or returns it against the checklist. Record review and correction time, not just
    drafting time.
    Acceptance test: Someone other than the instructor can run it; authority is clear; sensitive
    information is excluded; sending or publishing still requires approval.
    Between-session assignment: Repeat the delegated workflow and run the first agent pilot
    task. Collect a before-and-after example and ask what supervision was still necessary.
Do not label owner review time as eliminated if it has merely moved from drafting to
correction. That distinction belongs in both the learning and the value calculation.
Week 7: Make onboarding repeatable without depending on the instructor
    Build: Configure a finished New Agent Onboarding Plan around the team's standards,
    responsibilities, checkpoints, and first 30 days of work. Teams actively recruiting but not
    onboarding may use the recruiting interview-preparation variant, with the same test
    standard.
    Skills: New Agent Onboarding Plan is the default flagship, supported by Seat Definition
    Writer. Recruiting Outreach and Interview is a bounded alternative, not an extra required
    build.
    Why it matters: This is a concrete way to “scale the unscalable”: encode how this team
    brings someone up to standard, while preserving the human coaching and decisions.
    How to use it: At a new hire or role transition, supply the approved role, experience,
    standards, and required resources; review the plan; assign checkpoints to real people.
    Live exercise: Pairs adapt the supplied package without a click-by-click walkthrough,
    exchange redacted tests, and explain one correction. Advanced participants can author a
    new component; beginners do not face a blank-page exam.
    Working at the end of class: A versioned skill with three tests: normal inputs, missing
    inputs, and a misleading or conflicting input. Members must show how it fails safely.
    Working by week’s end: A second person follows the runbook and reproduces an
    acceptable result. If there is no current recruit or new hire, label it “tested and ready,” not
    “adopted” or “saving time.”
    Acceptance test: The team can explain the trigger, required data, human judgment points,
    and repair procedure without the instructor taking over.
    Between-session assignment: Finish the two-agent pilot, gather actual output and
    supervision evidence, and prepare the week 8 operating review.
For recruiting, keep outreach in draft form and keep candidate decisions human-owned against
consistent job-related criteria. For onboarding, assess a useful first 30 days before adding detail
to days 60 and 90.

Week 8: Prove what works and plan the next quarter
    Build: Assemble the cohort evidence into an operating review and 90-day continuation
    plan. Use the EOS Quarterly Packet structure where appropriate, but call this a cohort-end
    review rather than pretending early March is the end of a calendar quarter.
    Skills: EOS Quarterly Packet, Weekly Production Report, the economics review, and the
    prior weeks’ logs. The agent pilot supplies evidence for rollout.
    Why it matters: The final week should prevent abandonment after the coaching ends. It
    should also help owners decide what not to scale.
    How to use it: Review which routines were used, which outputs were accepted, what
    changed, what broke, and what remains unproven. Assign maintenance and the next
    monthly review.
    Working at the end of class: A concise evidence-backed scorecard; a keep/fix/retire
    decision for each workflow; and a 90-day plan with owners, dates, and measures.
    Working by week’s end: Final corrections completed, skills and runbooks handed off,
    support boundaries understood, and the first post-cohort operating review on the calendar.
    Acceptance test: Every benefit claim is labeled measured, estimated, or unverified; the
    maintainer can run the core workflows; agent rollout has a genuine go/no-go decision.
    Final assignment: Commit to the next four weekly reviews. A future paid service must not
    be required for the owner’s existing workflows to keep functioning.
Use approximately 10 minutes for the results review, 20 for assembling the packet, 25 for
owner/operator decisions, 20 for peer teach-backs in staffed groups, 10 for agent rollout
choices, and 5 for final commitments. Do not attempt a full-length public presentation from
every team inside a 90-minute session.

Beginner and advanced paths
Use Brooke's existing proficiency rubric rather than inventing another public label. Run one
business lesson and one required output, then vary the implementation challenge; no
participant should be penalized for needing the supplied scaffold.
    Core path: Preconfigured skill, annotated input example, minimum-data build, and one
    reviewed real-world use.
    Advanced path: The same business outcome, plus a harder reconciliation, edge-case test,
    or safely scoped connection. Do not reward sophistication with an unrelated second
    curriculum.
    Owner requirement: Explain one financial or operating conclusion and approve an action.
    The operator cannot complete this element on the owner's behalf.
    Operator requirement: Refresh the input, reproduce an output, identify a failure, and
    follow the repair procedure.
At Scottsdale, use the smaller Sisu boardroom only if a second facilitator and a parallel run-of-

should not become unpaid technical support for the main room.

The 60-minute office hour
    First 10 minutes: Patterns in submitted failures and fixes that apply to the whole room.
    Next 40 minutes: Four approximately ten-minute, pre-triaged cases. Favor common
    blockers and rotate access; do not promise everyone an individual hot seat every week.
    Final 10 minutes: Retesting, commitments, and the shared support log.
Require a short issue submission: expected result, actual result, redacted input, attempted fix,
and platform. Avoid spending 20 minutes discovering that a participant does not have export
permission.
Office hours remain open and optional. The feedback loop is not optional: every team receives
its standardized rubric status whether or not it attends, with substantive teaching and
troubleshooting handled in the group.
Library buyers may observe a designated redacted general-help segment during their included
30 days, if that access is explicitly promised. Do not use private cohort cases as an upsell
demonstration without consent, and do not let library traffic consume the cohort's guaranteed
troubleshooting time.
The November-sale timing matters: 30 days of library office hours starting immediately would
expire in December, before January cohort calls. To use shared calls as the intended bridge,
explicitly offer a January support activation or a defined extension; otherwise provide the
November/December support sold and treat the upsell mechanism as separate. Do not silently
defer purchased support.

The weekly evidence packet
Keep the submission small enough to sustain: a skill/version link, one redacted output, its test
checklist, a use log, and a three-sentence account of the action taken. Permit a two-minute
recording instead of a long written explanation.

The AI environment
Keep the business definition of a skill portable: trigger, required inputs, instructions, output
format, quality checks, approval boundaries, and tests. A portable specification is not the same
thing as a universally compatible executable package.
Claude’s current documentation supports custom uploaded skills, requires code execution, and
warns that skills may introduce prompt-injection or data-exfiltration risks (Claude skills
documentation). That is a practical reason to use a reviewed, versioned curriculum package
rather than having participants install arbitrary shared skills.
For the ChatGPT or Grok lanes, test the equivalent saved-instruction workflow and actual tool
permissions instead of asserting feature parity. A manually invoked workflow is acceptable;
unattended execution and cross-platform package compatibility are not part of the default
promise.
OpenAI says that business-product inputs and outputs are excluded from model training by
default, but that does not by itself establish a team’s compliance or authorize every data upload
(OpenAI business-data policy). Require an approved data-handling configuration for whichever
provider the team chooses, and recheck terms and settings before using live client or employee
information.

The economics workflow: the essential missing piece

What to calculate
Use a deterministic spreadsheet or reviewed calculation routine for arithmetic. Let AI explain
the results, identify missing inputs, and draft decision questions; require the explanation to
reference the calculated values.
     Cost per lead: Defined acquisition spend divided by unique leads created for the relevant
     source and period. Define whether staff costs or only direct marketing costs are included.
     Period cost per closing: Source acquisition spend during a period divided by source-
     attributed closings during that period. Label it as a period indicator, not the actual
     acquisition cost of the leads that closed.
     Cohort conversion: Closings arising from a specified lead-created cohort divided by
     eligible leads in that same cohort. State the follow-up window and how many leads remain
     immature.
     Team-retained commission: Begin with the team’s defined commission revenue and
     subtract applicable agent compensation, referral fees, concessions, and other clearly
     identified deductions without double-counting.
     Source contribution: Team-retained commission less included acquisition and other
     attributable variable costs, with the period/cohort basis explicit.
     Net profit: Only use the term when the accounting basis and full expense scope support it.
     An allocated source view must disclose the overhead-allocation rule.
If the expense or attribution data is incomplete, show “unknown,” “incomplete,” or
“provisional.” Do not set missing spend to zero, treat unknown lead source as a real marketing
channel, or compare sources that use different inclusion rules.

The required checks
     Tie-out: Compare production totals with the designated production report and revenue
     with the relevant accounting report; explain timing differences rather than forcing a
     match.
     Period: Document cash/accrual basis where applicable, reporting dates, extraction date,
     and lead-to-close lag.
     Coverage: Show the proportion of records and dollars with usable attribution. Do not hide
     unmatched records from the total.
     Sample size: Avoid strong budget conclusions from one closing or a very recent lead
     cohort.
     Cost scope: State which costs are included, excluded, allocated, or unavailable.
     Decision: End with “continue,” “investigate,” or “test a change,” including a human owner
     and review date.
A good week 1 result may be: “We cannot yet compare these two sources fairly because referral
fees are missing from one and the second has only a recent lead cohort.” That is useful
management judgment, but do not market incomplete analysis as a completed profit engine.

Reliability and privacy gate
    Rehearse: Run all three candidates end to end with the exact account, model, permissions,
    and presentation setup. Complete multiple successful runs plus one deliberate failure test
    before selecting the final sequence.
    Reconcile: Independently check every number that will appear on screen and keep the
    calculation visible.
    Protect: Use approved sanitized data or explicitly authorized demonstration data; never
    expose other teams' client lists, P&Ls, agent details, or connector credentials to the room.
    Prepare: Keep snapshots, sample inputs, expected outputs, and a short recorded fallback
    ready.
    Disclose: Say when you switch to a snapshot or recording. After one brief failed retry, move
    on rather than debugging authentication on stage.
    Separate scope: Draft outputs only by default. Do not add a live CRM merge, bulk send,
    financial write, or task import just to make the demonstration feel more impressive.
Saturday shows one useful run. The paid curriculum supplies the team's actual mappings,

Use the member spotlights as attributed demonstrations of experience, not independently

Bottom line
The course is worth building if you are willing to own implementation quality, not just deliver
content. Your strongest product is an owner/operator pair that knows its business better, uses a
small set of workflows consistently, and can improve them without depending on you.
Give members the finished tools, then make the business thinking substantive, the
implementation checks dependable, and the benefit claims conservative. That is a stronger
foundation for the working $5,000 offer than either adding more skills or making simple builds
feel artificially complicated.
